### 🟦 Aqueous Copper(II) Reactions

>[!info] Copper(II) + Dropwise NaOH or NH₃ (Acid-Base / Precipitation)
>**Equation**
>- `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O`
>- *Or with ammonia:* `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2NH₃ → [Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2NH₄⁺`
>
>**Observations**
>- **Pale blue solution** forms a **pale blue precipitate**.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* This is **not** a ligand exchange. It is an **acid-base / deprotonation** reaction. The base removes protons from the water ligands. 
>- ***Inference: You may be asked why the precipitate forms. Answer: The complex loses its charge (becomes neutral), so it can no longer form strong ion-dipole bonds with the water solvent.***

>[!info] Copper(II) + Excess NH₃ (Ligand Exchange)
>**Equation**
>- `[Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 2H₂O + 2OH⁻`
>- *Overall from hexaaqua:* `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- Pale blue precipitate dissolves to form a **deep/dark blue solution**.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Note that only **four** water ligands are replaced by ammonia. The final complex is still **octahedral**, but the bonds are distorted (Jahn-Teller effect, though you don't need to name it). Do not draw a square planar copper-ammonia complex unless explicitly forced to!
>- *Type of reaction:* Ligand exchange.

>[!info] Copper(II) + Concentrated HCl (Ligand Exchange & Shape Change)
>**Equation**
>- `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- Pale blue solution turns into a **yellow solution**. 
>- *(Note: Often appears green during the transition because blue + yellow = green, but the final mark scheme answer is yellow).*
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* The coordination number changes from **6 to 4**. 
>- The geometry changes from **octahedral to tetrahedral**.
>- ***Inference: Why does the coordination number drop to 4? Because the chloride ion (Cl⁻) is a much larger ligand than H₂O, so only four can physically fit around the central Cu²⁺ ion without extreme steric hindrance/repulsion.*** This reaction is easily reversible by adding water.

### 🟪 Aqueous Cobalt(II) Reactions

>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Dropwise NaOH or NH₃ (Acid-Base / Precipitation)
>**Equation**
>- `[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → [Co(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 2H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- **Pink solution** forms a **blue precipitate** (which may turn pink/red on standing, but "blue ppt" is the standard required answer).
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Just like copper, this is an **acid-base/deprotonation** reaction, NOT a ligand exchange. 

>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Excess NH₃ (Ligand Exchange)
>**Equation**
>- `[Co(H₂O)₄(OH)₂] + 6NH₃ → [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ + 4H₂O + 2OH⁻`
>- *Overall from hexaaqua:* `[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 6NH₃ → [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ + 6H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- Blue precipitate dissolves to form a **straw / yellow-brown solution**.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Unlike Copper, Cobalt replaces **all six** water ligands with ammonia. 
>- ***Inference: If this yellow-brown solution is left to stand in air, it darkens to a brown/black solution. Why? Because the Co²⁺ complex is easily oxidised by oxygen in the air to form a stable Co³⁺ complex: `[Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺`.***

>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Concentrated HCl (Ligand Exchange & Shape Change)
>**Equation**
>- `[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- **Pink solution** turns into a **blue solution**.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Just like copper, the coordination number changes from **6 to 4**, and geometry shifts from **octahedral to tetrahedral**. 
>- ***Inference: This is an endothermic equilibrium. You might be given Le Chatelier scenarios: heating the mixture turns it deeper blue (shifts right), adding water turns it back to pink (shifts left).***

### ⬜ Aqueous Silver(I) Reactions

>[!info] Tollens' Reagent Preparation (Precipitation & Ligand Exchange)
>**Equations**
>- Step 1 (Add NaOH): `2Ag⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ag₂O(s) + H₂O` 
>- Step 2 (Add excess NH₃): `Ag₂O(s) + 4NH₃ + H₂O → 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 2OH⁻`
>
>**Observations**
>- Step 1: **Brown precipitate** of Silver(I) oxide forms.
>- Step 2: Precipitate dissolves to form a **colourless solution**.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Silver does not form a stable hydroxide. `AgOH` spontaneously dehydrates into `Ag₂O`. Do not write `AgOH` as the precipitate. 
>- The final complex, `[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺`, is **linear** with an N-Ag-N bond angle of **180°**.
>- ***Inference: Why is `[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺` colourless? Because Ag⁺ is a d¹⁰ ion. Its d-subshell is completely full, meaning no d-d electron promotion can occur, hence no visible light is absorbed.***

### 🔴 Redox Reactions (Titrations)

>[!info] Manganate(VII) & Iron(II) Titration (Redox)
>**Equation**
>- `MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- **Purple** solution (MnO₄⁻) turns to **colourless/very pale pink** (Mn²⁺) as it reacts.
>- *End-point:* The first permanent pale pink colour.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Ratio is strictly **1 MnO₄⁻ : 5 Fe²⁺**. 
>- Must be done in acidic conditions (usually dilute H₂SO₄). Do not use HCl (MnO₄⁻ will oxidise Cl⁻ to Cl₂ gas) or HNO₃ (it is an oxidising agent itself).

>[!info] Manganate(VII) & Ethanedioate Titration (Redox & Autocatalysis)
>**Equation**
>- `2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ → 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O`
>
>**Observations**
>- **Purple** solution turns **colourless**. Effervescence (CO₂ gas) is observed.
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Ratio is strictly **2 MnO₄⁻ : 5 C₂O₄²⁻**.
>- **Requires heating (~60°C).** 
>- ***Inference: You may be asked why the reaction starts slow but speeds up. This is AUTOCATALYSIS. The product, Mn²⁺, acts as a homogeneous catalyst for the reaction.***

>[!info] Copper(II) & Iodide Reaction (Redox)
>**Equation**
>- `2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI(s) + I₂(aq)`
>
>**Observations**
>- Blue solution turns into a **brown solution** (due to aqueous I₂) containing a **white precipitate** (CuI).
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* The precipitate is often masked by the brown iodine, so it looks like a murky brown mixture. 
>- ***Inference: The generated Iodine is then titrated with Sodium Thiosulfate (`I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻`). Therefore, 1 mole of S₂O₃²⁻ is equivalent to 1 mole of Cu²⁺. This 1:1 ratio is a highly tested calculation shortcut!***

### ⚙️ Homogeneous Catalysis Reactions

>[!info] Iron Catalysis of Iodide & Peroxodisulfate (S₂O₈²⁻)
>**Equations**
>- Uncatalysed: `S₂O₈²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO₄²⁻ + I₂` (Very slow!)
>- Catalysed Step 1: `S₂O₈²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO₄²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺`
>- Catalysed Step 2: `2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂`
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Why is the uncatalysed reaction so slow? Because it involves the collision of **two highly negatively charged ions** (S₂O₈²⁻ and I⁻), which strongly repel each other, resulting in a very high activation energy ($E_a$). 
>- The catalyst works because the Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ ions are positively charged and easily attract the negative reactant ions. 
>- ***Inference: You can start the mechanism with Fe³⁺ instead of Fe²⁺ (i.e., swap the order of step 1 and 2), and it is perfectly valid! The standard electrode potential ($E^\circ$) of Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺ sits right between the $E^\circ$ of the two reactants, allowing it to act as an intermediate electron carrier.***

>[!info] Nitrogen Dioxide (NO₂) Catalysis of Acid Rain Production
>**Equations**
>- Uncatalysed: `SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃`
>- Catalysed Step 1: `SO₂ + NO₂ → SO₃ + NO`
>- Catalysed Step 2: `NO + ½O₂ → NO₂`
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* This is **homogeneous catalysis** because the catalyst (NO₂ gas) and the reactants (SO₂, O₂ gases) are in the **same physical state/phase**.
>- The SO₃ then dissolves in rainwater to form highly corrosive sulfuric acid (`SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄`).

### 🔗 Multidentate Ligand Exchange (The Chelate Effect)

>[!info] Ligand Substitution with EDTA⁴⁻ or Ethanedioate (C₂O₄²⁻)
>**Equation Example**
>- `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + EDTA⁴⁻ ⇌ [Cu(EDTA)]²⁻ + 6H₂O`
>
>**Notes & Nuances**
>- *Examiner Nuance:* Why does this reaction happen so readily? Because of the **Chelate Effect** (an entropy-driven phenomenon). 
>- **Explanation to memorize:** 2 particles on the left side of the equation become 7 particles on the right side. This causes a massive **increase in the disorder/entropy ($\Delta S$)** of the system. 
>- Since $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, a large positive $\Delta S$ makes $\Delta G$ highly negative, meaning the forward reaction is highly feasible and the stability constant ($K_{stab}$) for the multidentate complex is astronomically large.
>- ***Inference: If asked to explain why multidentate complexes are "more stable" than monodentate ones, ALWAYS base your answer on the increase in entropy ($\Delta S > 0$) releasing water molecules into the solvent bulk.***
