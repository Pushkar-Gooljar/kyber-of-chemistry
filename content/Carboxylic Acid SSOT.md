## 📌 Part 1: AS Level Foundations

### 1.1 Structure and Physical Properties
Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl functional group (**-COOH**). 

> [!note] Physical Properties & Dimerisation
> - **Boiling Points:** Carboxylic acids have higher boiling points than corresponding alcohols and alkanes. This is because they can form **extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding**.
> - **Dimerisation:** In non-polar solvents (or in the gas phase), carboxylic acids form **dimers**. Two molecules hydrogen-bond together, effectively doubling the apparent relative molecular mass ($M_r$). *E.g., Ethanoic acid ($M_r$ 60) behaves as if its $M_r$ is 120.*
> - **Solubility:** Soluble in water up to 4 carbon atoms due to hydrogen bonding with water. As the non-polar hydrocarbon chain lengthens, solubility rapidly decreases.

### 1.2 Preparation of Carboxylic Acids
There are three main synthetic routes to produce carboxylic acids at AS Level:

#### 1. Oxidation of Primary Alcohols and Aldehydes
- **Reagents:** Acidified Potassium Dichromate(VI) ($K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+$) OR Acidified Potassium Manganate(VII) ($KMnO_4/H^+$)
- **Conditions:** Heat under **reflux**.
- **Observation (Dichromate):** Orange solution turns **green** ($Cr_2O_7^{2-} \rightarrow Cr^{3+}$).
- **Observation (Manganate):** Purple solution turns **colourless** ($MnO_4^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+}$).

#### 2. Hydrolysis of Nitriles (-C≡N)
Nitriles can be hydrolysed using either dilute acids or dilute alkalis.
- **Acid Hydrolysis:** Heat under reflux with dilute acid (e.g., $HCl_{(aq)}$ or $H_2SO_4_{(aq)}$).
	- *Products:* Carboxylic acid + Ammonium salt ($NH_4^+$).
	- *Equation:* $R-CN + 2H_2O + H^+ \rightarrow R-COOH + NH_4^+$
- **Alkaline Hydrolysis:** Heat under reflux with dilute alkali (e.g., $NaOH_{(aq)}$).
	- *Products:* Carboxylate salt ($R-COO^-Na^+$) + Ammonia gas ($NH_3$).
	- *Crucial Step:* You must add a strong dilute acid (acidification) afterward to protonate the carboxylate ion and release the free carboxylic acid.

#### 3. Hydrolysis of Esters
- Similar to nitriles, esters can be hydrolysed using dilute acid (reversible, equilibrium mixture) or dilute alkali (goes to completion, forms carboxylate salt, requires acidification). *See Section 1.4 for details.*

### 1.3 Reactions of Carboxylic Acids
Carboxylic acids are **weak acids**, meaning they partially dissociate in water: $RCOOH \rightleftharpoons RCOO^- + H^+$

> [!warning] Mark Scheme Trap: Reactions with Metals/Bases
> Examiners often test your ability to differentiate the acidity of Alcohols, Phenols, and Carboxylic Acids. 
> - **Alcohols** react ONLY with reactive metals (Na).
> - **Phenols** react with metals (Na) AND strong bases (NaOH).
> - **Carboxylic Acids** react with metals, strong bases, AND **carbonates** ($Na_2CO_3$). *Effervescence ($CO_2$) with carbonates is the standard test for a carboxylic acid.*

1. **Redox with Reactive Metals:** Forms salt + $H_2(g)$
2. **Neutralisation with Alkalis:** Forms salt + $H_2O(l)$
3. **Acid-Base with Carbonates:** Forms salt + $H_2O(l) + CO_2(g)$
4. **Reduction:**
   - **Reagent:** Lithium aluminium hydride ($LiAlH_4$).
   - **Conditions:** In **dry ether** at room temperature.
   - **Product:** Primary alcohol.
   - *Trap:* $NaBH_4$ is **not strong enough** to reduce carboxylic acids!
5. **Esterification:**
   - **Reagents:** Alcohol + Carboxylic acid.
   - **Conditions:** Heat under reflux with a **concentrated $H_2SO_4$ catalyst**.
   - *Note:* This is a reversible condensation reaction.

## 🚀 Part 2: A Level (A2) Advanced Concepts

### 2.1 Preparation of Benzoic Acid (Aromatic)
- **Reaction:** Oxidation of the alkyl side-chain of alkylbenzenes (e.g., methylbenzene).
- **Reagents:** Hot alkaline $KMnO_4$ followed by acidification with dilute acid (e.g., $HCl$).
- **Observation:** Purple solution decolorises, and a white precipitate of benzoic acid forms upon acidification.
- **Key Concept:** Regardless of the length of the alkyl chain (ethyl, propyl, etc.), it is entirely cleaved and oxidised down to a single -COOH group attached to the ring.

### 2.2 Further Oxidation of Specific Carboxylic Acids
Normally, carboxylic acids cannot be further oxidised. However, there are **two exceptions** you must know:

#### 1. Methanoic Acid ($HCOOH$)
Because methanoic acid has a hydrogen atom attached directly to the carbonyl group (making it look like an aldehyde), it acts as a strong reducing agent.
- **Oxidised to:** $CO_2 + H_2O$
- **Reagents:** Can be oxidised by strong agents ($KMnO_4/H^+$, $K_2Cr_2O_7/H^+$) AND mild agents (**Tollens' reagent**, **Fehling's solution**).
- *Observation:* Gives a silver mirror with Tollens' and a brick-red precipitate with Fehling's.

#### 2. Ethanedioic Acid ($HOOC-COOH$)
- **Oxidised to:** $2CO_2 + H_2O$
- **Reagents:** Warm acidified $KMnO_4$.
- **Reaction:** $5H_2C_2O_4 + 2MnO_4^- + 6H^+ \rightarrow 10CO_2 + 2Mn^{2+} + 8H_2O$
- **Examiner Note:** This reaction is used in redox titrations. It is an example of **autocatalysis**, where the $Mn^{2+}$ products act as a catalyst for the reaction.

### 2.3 The "Why" of Acidity (High-Yield Exam Topic)
This is one of the most frequently tested explanation questions in Paper 4. You must use specific terminology.

#### A. Relative Acidities: Carboxylic Acids > Phenol > Water > Alcohols
*Why are carboxylic acids stronger acids than phenols and alcohols?*
1. When a carboxylic acid donates a proton, it forms a **carboxylate anion** ($R-COO^-$).
2. The negative charge is **delocalised** over **two highly electronegative oxygen atoms**.
3. This greatly **reduces the charge density** of the anion.
4. Therefore, the carboxylate anion is highly **stabilised**, making it much less likely to attract the $H^+$ ion back (equilibrium lies further to the right).

*Why is phenol weaker than carboxylic acids but stronger than alcohols?*
- The phenoxide ion's negative charge is delocalised into the benzene ring, which stabilises it, but carbon is less electronegative than oxygen, so it is less stable than a carboxylate ion. 
- Alcohols have electron-donating alkyl groups (positive inductive effect), which *increase* the charge density on the alkoxide ion, destabilising it.

#### B. Substituted Carboxylic Acids
Adding electronegative atoms (like halogens) to the alkyl chain changes the acid strength via the **inductive effect**.

> [!success] The Mark Scheme Formula for Substituted Acids
> **1. Effect of Electron-Withdrawing Groups (e.g., Cl, F):**
> Electronegative atoms exert a **negative inductive effect**, pulling electron density away from the -COOH group. This **weakens the O-H bond** and **stabilises the resulting carboxylate anion** by spreading out the negative charge. *Result: Stronger acid.*
> 
> **2. Effect of Number of Substituents:**
> Trichloroethanoic acid > Dichloroethanoic acid > Chloroethanoic acid. *(More electron-withdrawing groups = greater stabilization of anion = stronger acid).*
> 
> **3. Effect of Distance:**
> 2-chlorobutanoic acid is stronger than 4-chlorobutanoic acid. The closer the electronegative atom is to the -COOH group, the stronger the inductive effect.
> 
> **4. Effect of Electron-Donating Groups (e.g., Alkyl chains):**
> Alkyl groups push electron density towards the -COOH group (**positive inductive effect**), destabilising the carboxylate anion by increasing its charge density. *Result: Weaker acid (Ethanoic acid is weaker than Methanoic acid).*

### 2.4 Acyl Chlorides ($R-COCl$)
Acyl chlorides are highly reactive derivatives of carboxylic acids.

#### 1. Preparation of Acyl Chlorides
You convert a carboxylic acid to an acyl chloride by replacing the -OH with a -Cl. 
- **Reagent 1:** $PCl_5$ (Solid, occurs at room temp. By-products: $POCl_3 + HCl_{(g)}$)
- **Reagent 2:** $PCl_3$ + Heat (Liquid. By-product: $H_3PO_3$)
- **Reagent 3:** $SOCl_2$ (Liquid, room temp. By-products: $SO_2_{(g)} + HCl_{(g)}$)
> [!tip] Best Synthetic Route
> Using $SOCl_2$ is preferred in industry because both by-products ($SO_2$ and $HCl$) are gases and easily escape the reaction mixture, leaving pure liquid acyl chloride.

#### 2. Reactions of Acyl Chlorides
Acyl chlorides react violently with nucleophiles. In all these reactions, **steamy white fumes of $HCl$ gas** are observed.

| Nucleophile | Reagent | Product | Conditions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Water** | $H_2O$ | Carboxylic Acid | Room temp (vigorous, highly exothermic) |
| **Alcohol** | $R-OH$ | Ester | Room temp (irreversible, goes to completion) |
| **Phenol** | Phenol + NaOH | Phenyl Ester | Warm. *Must use NaOH first to form the phenoxide ion (a better nucleophile) because phenol itself is a poor nucleophile.* |
| **Ammonia** | $NH_3$ | Primary Amide | Room temp, concentrated $NH_3$. |
| **Primary Amine**| $R-NH_2$ | Secondary Amide | Room temp. |

> [!warning] Stoichiometry Trap with Amines/Ammonia
> When reacting acyl chlorides with ammonia or amines, **2 moles** of the amine/ammonia are required per mole of acyl chloride. 
> - Mole 1 acts as the nucleophile to form the amide.
> - Mole 2 acts as a base to neutralize the highly acidic $HCl$ by-product, forming an ammonium salt (e.g., $CH_3NH_3^+Cl^-$).

#### 3. Mechanism: Nucleophilic Addition-Elimination
Acyl chlorides react via **Addition-Elimination** (often termed *condensation*). 
1. **Addition:** The lone pair on the nucleophile (e.g., Oxygen in water) attacks the extremely highly $\delta+$ carbonyl carbon. The $\pi$-bond of the $C=O$ breaks, pushing electrons onto the Oxygen, giving it a full negative charge (forming a tetrahedral intermediate).
2. **Elimination:** The lone pair on the negative Oxygen collapses back down to re-form the $C=O$ double bond, which forces the $C-Cl$ bond to break, expelling the chloride ion ($Cl^-$) as a leaving group. A proton is then lost to form $HCl$.

> [!abstract] Extra Context (Not strictly syllabus, but aids understanding)
> Why are Acyl Chlorides so reactive? The carbonyl carbon is attached to *two* highly electronegative atoms (O and Cl). Both pull electron density away, leaving the central carbon intensely electron-deficient ($\delta+$) and incredibly susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

#### 4. Relative Ease of Hydrolysis (The Chlorine Compounds)
Examiners love asking you to compare the hydrolysis rates of Acyl Chlorides, Alkyl Chlorides (halogenoalkanes), and Aryl Chlorides (chlorobenzene).

**Order of reactivity (Ease of hydrolysis):**
`Acyl Chloride > Alkyl Chloride > Aryl Chloride`

**The Mark Scheme Explanation:**
- **Acyl Chloride (Most Reactive):** The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two strongly electronegative atoms (O and Cl), making it highly $\delta+$ and very attractive to nucleophiles. Reaction occurs rapidly at room temperature with neutral water.
- **Alkyl Chloride (Intermediate):** Carbon is bonded to only one electronegative atom (Cl). Less $\delta+$. Requires a strong nucleophile ($OH^-$) and heat under reflux to hydrolyse.
- **Aryl Chloride / Chlorobenzene (Unreactive):** A lone pair of electrons on the Chlorine atom delocalises into the $\pi$-electron system of the benzene ring. This gives the $C-Cl$ bond **partial double-bond character**, making it extremely strong and resistant to nucleophilic attack/hydrolysis.

## 🧠 Examiner's Vault: Top P2 & P4 Traps

1. **Esterification: Acyl Chloride vs. Carboxylic Acid**
   *Question:* "State two advantages of using an acyl chloride rather than a carboxylic acid to make an ester."
   *MS Answer:* 
   2. The reaction goes to completion (higher yield) whereas carboxylic acid esterification is an equilibrium. 
   3. It occurs at room temperature / requires no heat. 
   4. It does not require a concentrated acid catalyst.

5. **Dicarboxylic Acids & Oxidation**
   *Question:* "A sample of ethanedioic acid is titrated with $KMnO_4$. The reaction is initially very slow but speeds up. Explain why."
   *MS Answer:* The reaction is autocatalysed by the $Mn^{2+}$ ions produced during the reaction. 

6. **Phenol + Acyl Chloride**
   *Question:* "Explain why NaOH is added to phenol before reacting it with an acyl chloride."
   *MS Answer:* Phenol reacts with NaOH to form the phenoxide ion ($C_6H_5O^-$). The phenoxide ion is a stronger/better nucleophile than phenol, allowing the addition-elimination reaction to occur more readily.

7. **Acidity Comparison Wording**
   *Never* just say "the ion is stable." *Always* explicitly state: "The negative charge is delocalised over the two electronegative oxygen atoms of the $COO^-$ group, which reduces charge density and stabilises the conjugate base."

8. **Observations**
   If a question asks for an observation when an acyl chloride reacts with water/alcohols, do not just write "reaction happens". You must write: **"Steamy white fumes (of HCl) are observed."**
