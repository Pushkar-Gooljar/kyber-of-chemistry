# Types of Half-Cells
>[!info] Types of Half-Cells
>**There are three types of half-cells that can be connected to a standard hydrogen electrode:**
>
>1. Metal / Metal ion half-cell
>2. Non-Metal / Non-Metal ion half-cell
>3. Ion / Ion half-cell (the ions are in different oxidation states)

>[!important] Representing Half-Cells
> When writing shorthand representations for individual half-cells (redox couples), the IUPAC convention dictates that the species with the **higher oxidation state (the oxidized form)** is always written on the left:
> 
> $$\text{Oxidized form} \ / \ \text{Reduced form} \quad (\text{Ox} \ / \ \text{Red})$$
> 
>**Why?** This order mirrors the direction of standard reduction half-equations ($\text{Ox} + ne^- \rightarrow \text{Red}$), which is how they are listed in $E^\ominus$ data tables.
>
 **Examples:**
>   * $\ce{Ag+/Ag}$ (Oxidation states: $+1 \rightarrow 0$)
>   * $\ce{Br2/Br-}$ (Oxidation states: $0 \rightarrow -1$)
>   * $\ce{Fe^{3+}/Fe^{2+}}$ (Oxidation states: $+3 \rightarrow +2$)
>

## 1. Metal / Metal Ion
>[!info] Metal / Metal Ion Half-Cell
>- The metal itself acts as the electrode.
>- It is dipped in $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ of its ions.

>[!concept] What happens to the metal electrode?
> The behavior of a metal electrode depends on whether it acts as the cathode or the anode in the electrochemical cell:
> 
> **If the electrode is the Cathode (Reduction):**
>   * Aqueous metal ions are reduced to solid metal: $$\ce{M^{n+}(aq) + $n$e- -> M(s)}$$
>   * **Result:** The concentration of aqueous ions **decreases**, while the mass of the metal electrode **increases**.
> 
> **If the electrode is the Anode (Oxidation):**
>   * The solid metal electrode is oxidized to aqueous ions: $$\ce{M(s) -> M^{n+}(aq) + $n$e-}$$
>   * **Result:** The concentration of aqueous ions **increases**, while the mass of the metal electrode **decreases**.

>[!example] Example: $\ce{Ag+(aq)}/\ce{Ag(s)}$ Half-Cell
> - **Electrode:** Silver metal ($\ce{Ag(s)}$)
> - **Solution:** $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{Ag+(aq)}$
> 
> When this half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)**, the two half-equations are:
> 
> $$ \ce{Ag+(aq) + e- <=> Ag(s)} \qquad E^\ominus = +0.80 \text{ V} $$
> $$ \ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- <=> H2(g)} \qquad E^\ominus = 0.00 \text{ V} $$
> 
> The potential difference between the two half-cells is:
> $$ E^\ominus_{\text{cell}} = (+0.80\text{ V}) - (0.00\text{ V}) = +0.80 \text{ V} $$
> 
> Therefore, the voltmeter reading will be $+0.80 \text{ V}$. 
> 
> ![Under standard conditions, a metal / metal ion half-cell is connected to the standard hydrogen electrode to measure the cell potential.|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/cdn-cgi/image/f=auto,width=1080/https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2025/06/28069_httpscdn-savemyexams-comuploads2021015-3-principles-of-electrochemistry-example-of-a-metal-metal-ion-half-cell.png)
> 
> Because the silver half-cell has the more positive $E^\ominus$ value, it acts as the **cathode** (reduction occurs). Over time, the concentration of $\ce{Ag+(aq)}$ will decrease, and the mass of the silver electrode will increase.

## 2. Non-Metal / Non-Metal Ion

>[!info] Non-Metal / Non-Metal Ion Half-Cell
>- Platinum is used as electrode to make electrical contact with the solution.
>- Gas is bubbled at 1 atm into a $1.00 \text{ mol dm}{-3}$ solution of the non-metal ions.

>[!example] $\ce{Br_{2}(g)}/\ce{Br-(aq)}$ Half-Cell
>- **Electrode:** Platinum
>- **Solution:** $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{Br-(aq)}$
>  
>
When this half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)**, the two half-equations are:
  $$\ce{Br_{2}(g) + 2e- \rightleftharpoons 2Br-(aq)} \qquad \mathrm{E^{⦵}}=+1.09 \text{ V}$$
  $$ \ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- <=> H2(g)} \qquad E^\ominus = 0.00 \text{ V} $$
  > The potential difference between the two half-cells is:
> $$ E^\ominus_{\text{cell}} = (+1.09\text{ V}) - (0.00\text{ V}) = +1.09\text{ V} $$
> 
> Therefore, the voltmeter reading will be $+1.09 \text{ V}$. 
> 
> ![Under standard conditions, a non-metal / non-metal ion half-cell is connected to the standard hydrogen electrode to measure the cell potential|500](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/cdn-cgi/image/f=auto,width=1080/https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2025/06/63417_httpscdn-savemyexams-comuploads2021015-3-principles-of-electrochemistry-example-of-a-non-metal-non-metal-ion-half-cell.png)

## 3. Ion / Ion (Different Oxidation States)


>[!info] Ion / Ion Half-Cell
>- Platinum is used as electrode to make electrical contact with the solution.
>- Both ions must be present in the solution at $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$. i.e. a mixture containing $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ of each ion.

>[!example] $\ce{MnO_{4}-(aq)}/\ce{Mn^2+(aq)}$ Half-Cell
>- **Electrode:** Platinum
>- **Solution:** $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{MnO_{4}-(aq)}$ + $1.00 \text{ mol dm}^{-3}$ $\ce{Mn^2+(aq)}$
> - $\ce{MnO_{4}-}$ contains $\ce{Mn}$ with oxidation number of $+7$
> - $\ce{Mn^2+}$ contains $\ce{Mn}$ with oxidation number of $+2$
>  
>
When this half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode (SHE)**, the two half-equations are:
  $$\ce{MnO_{4}-(aq) + 8H+(aq) + 5e- \rightleftharpoons Mn^2+(aq) + 4H_{2}O(l)} \qquad \mathrm{E^{⦵}}=+1.52 \text{ V}$$
  $$ \ce{2H+(aq) + 2e- <=> H2(g)} \qquad E^\ominus = 0.00 \text{ V} $$
  > The potential difference between the two half-cells is:
> $$ E^\ominus_{\text{cell}} = (+1.52\text{ V}) - (0.00\text{ V}) = +1.52\text{ V} $$
> 
> Therefore, the voltmeter reading will be $+1.52 \text{ V}$. 
>

# Salt Bridge
>[!concept] Salt Bridge
>When a half-cell is connected to a **standard hydrogen electrode**, or when two half-cells are connected, a **salt bridge** is required:
>- A salt bridge complete the circuit by allowing **mobile ions** to flow between the two half-cells.
>- A salt bridge is typically a strip of filter paper soaked in a saturated solution of **potassium nitrate** ($\ce{KNO_{3}}$).
>  
>  ![A salt bridge completing the circuit between two half-cells.|500](https://s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/elements.cognitoedu.org/031368d8-20e7-4689-91a1-e47e23245a8a/standard-hydrogen-electrode-diagram.png)


>[!important] Why is Potassium Nitrate ($\ce{KNO_{3}}$) used?
>**1. Universal Solubility of Nitrates**
>- All nitrates are soluble, this ensures that no precipitate forms which would alter ion concentrations and shift the equilibrium.
>  
>  
>**2. Similar Ionic Mobility**
>- $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{NO_{3}-}$ have almost identical sizes when hydrated and move at the exact same speed.
>- If one ion moved faster than the other, positive or negative charge would build up at the ends of the salt bridge, creating a disruptive voltage (liquid junction potential) which would interfere with the voltmeter reading.
