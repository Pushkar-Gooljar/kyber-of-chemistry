# Electrolysis

> [!info] Electrolysis
> **Electrolysis** is the chemical decomposition of an **electrolyte** (a molten ionic compound or an aqueous solution of ions) by the passage of a direct electric current (DC).

## Electrode Mechanisms and Electron Flow

An electrolytic cell uses an external power source to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. The process at each electrode is detailed below:

>[!concept] Cathode (Negative Electrode)
>- **Electron Flow:** Electrons flow from the negative terminal of the external power supply to this electrode, giving it a **negative charge**.
>- **Ion Migration:** Positive Ions (cations) in the electrolyte are attracted to this negative electrode.
>- **Chemical Process:** Cations gain electrons from the electrode surface:
>	- Gaining electrons is **reduction** (OIL RIG).
>	- Because reduction always occurs here, this electrode is defined as the **cathode** (Red Cat).

>[!concept] Anode (Positive Electrode)
>- **Electron Flow:** The positive terminal of the power supply withdraws electrons from this electrode, leaving it with a **positive charge**.
>- **Ion Migration:** Negative ions (anions) in the electrolyte are attracted to this positive electrode.
>- **Chemical Process:** Anions lose electrons to the electrode surface. These electrons travel back into the external circuit to complete the loop.
>	- Losing electrons is **oxidation** (OIL RIG).
>	- Because oxidation always occurs here, this electrode is defined as the **anode** (An Ox).

>[!tip]
>**Anode and Cathode**
>- Anode and Cathode are defined in terms of redox and not polarity or **an**ion or **cat**ion.
>- The anode will always be the electrode where **oxidation** takes place.
>- The cathode will always be the electrode where **reduction** takes place.
>
>**Mnemonics to remember:** 
>1. Red Cat (Reduction cathode)
>2. An Ox (Anode oxidation)

# Predicting Electrolysis Products
>[!info] Factors Affecting Electrolysis Products
>The products that will be formed at the anode and cathode depend on:
>1. **State of the electrolyte** (Molten or Aqueous)
>2. **Position in Redox Series** (Preferential discharge)
>3. **Concentration**

## 1. Electrolysis of Molten Electrolyte
>[!info] Electrolysis of a Molten Ionic Compound
>A molten ionic compound contains only two types of ions: 
>
>**1. Cation (positive)**
>- Attracted to cathode (negative electrode).
>- Gain electrons and is **reduced**.
>  
>**2. Anion (negative)**
>- Attracted to anode (positive electrode).
>- Lose electrons and is **oxidised**.
> 

>[!example] Example: Electrolysis of Molten $\ce{NaCl}$
>**Ions present:**
>1. Cation: $\ce{Na+}$
>2. Anion: $\ce{Cl-}$
>   
> **At Anode (+ve)**
> - $\ce{Cl-}$ ions are attracted to the anode.
> - They lose electrons and are oxidised to $\ce{Cl_{2}}$ gas:
> $$\ce{2Cl-(l) -> Cl_{2}(g) + 2e-}$$
> - **Observations:** Bubbles of pale yellow chlorine gas are evolved.
>
>**At Cathode (-ve)**
>- $\ce{Na+}$ ions are attracted tot he anode.
>- They gain one electron and are reduced to $\ce{Na}$ metal:
>$$\ce{Na+(l) + e- -> Na(s)}$$
>- **Observations:** Silvery liquid sodium metal forms.


## 2. Electrolysis of Aqueous Electrolytes

>[!info] Preferential Discharge in Aqueous Electrolysis
>Unlike molten electrolysis, **aqueous electrolysis** contains water molecules which partially dissociate to produce cations ($\ce{H+}$) and anions ($\ce{OH-}$):
>$$\ce{H_{2}O(l) \rightleftharpoons H+(aq) + OH-(aq)}$$
>
>Because there are competing ions at each electrode, we must apply the rules of **preferential discharge** to predict which species will actually react. Two factors determine this:
>1. **Standard Electrode Potential ($\mathrm{E^{⦵}}$):** The ease of reduction/oxidation of a species.
>2. **Relative Concentration:** High concentrations of certain ions can override standard electrode potential preferences.

---

### 1. Cation Discharge Order (At the Cathode)

>[!concept] Reduction at Cathode
>- The cathode is where **reduction occurs.**
>- Cations compete to gain electrons to be reduced.
>- The cation with the **most positive (least negative) $\mathrm{E^{⦵}}$ value** has the greatest tendency to be reduced and is preferentially discharged.

### 2. Anion Discharge Order (At the Anode)

>[!concept] Oxidation at Anode
>- The anode is where **oxidation** occurs.
>- Anions compete to lose electrons to be oxidised.
>- The anion with the **more negative (less positive) $\mathrm{E^{⦵}}$ value** has the greatest tendency to be oxidised and is preferentially discharged.

### 📊 Anion Discharge Series

| Mnemonic    | Ion                | Standard Reduction Half-Equation                                                                                   | $E^\theta$ Value (V) | Ease of Discharge                              |
| :---------- | :----------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| **S**end    | $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ | $\text{S}_2\text{O}_8^{2-}\text{(aq)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_4^{2-}\text{(aq)}$               | $+2.01$              | **Most Difficult** (Water is oxidised instead) |
| **N**oodles | $\text{NO}_3^-$    | *N/A (Nitrogen is already in maximum $+5$ state)*                                                                  | Highly Positive      |                                                |
| **C**hinese | $\text{Cl}^-$      | $\text{Cl}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{Cl}^-\text{(aq)}$                                   | $+1.36$              |                                                |
| **O**r      | $\text{OH}^-$      | **$\text{O}_2\text{(g)} + 4\text{H}^+\text{(aq)} + 4\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{(l)}$** | **$+1.23$**          |                                                |
| **B**ritish | $\ce{Br-}$         | $\text{Br}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{Br}^-\text{(aq)}$                                   | $+1.07$              |                                                |
| **I**nstead | $\ce{I-}$          | $\text{I}_2\text{(g)} + 2\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons 2\text{I}^-\text{(aq)}$                                     | $+0.54$              | **Easiest** (Most readily oxidised)            |

> [!important] 
> - Because sulfate and nitrate are extremely stable (very high $\mathrm{E^{⦵}}$, they are never oxidised at the anode. Water is oxidised instead, producing **oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$)**:
> $$2\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{(l)} \rightarrow \text{O}_2\text{(g)} + 4\text{H}^+\text{(aq)} + 4\text{e}^-$$
> - In aqueous solution, ions below $\ce{OH-}$ are preferentially discharged.

## 3. The Concentration Exception

When performing electrolysis on an aqueous solution, the concentration of the electrolyte can change which ion is preferentially discharged at the electrodes. The classic example is the **electrolysis of aqueous sodium chloride (NaCl)**.

### A. Dilute Aqueous NaCl
In a dilute solution, we must compare the standard electrode potentials ($E^\theta$) of the competing reactions at the **anode** (oxidation):

1. **Oxidation of Chloride:** 
   $$Cl_2(g) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2Cl^-(aq) \quad E^\theta = +1.36 \text{ V}$$
2. **Oxidation of Water:** 
   $$O_2(g) + 4H^+(aq) + 4e^- \rightleftharpoons 2H_2O(l) \quad E^\theta = +1.23 \text{ V}$$

* **Rule:** The species with the *less positive* $E$ value is easier to oxidise. 
* **Outcome:** Because $+1.23\text{ V} < +1.36\text{ V}$, water is preferentially oxidised. The product at the anode is **Oxygen gas ($O_2$)**.

---

### B. Concentrated Aqueous NaCl (Brine)
When the concentration of $NaCl$ is significantly increased, the rules change due to Le Chatelier's Principle.

Take the chlorine half-equation:
$$Cl_2(g) + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2Cl^-(aq)$$

1. **Applying LCP:** Increasing the concentration of $Cl^-$ ions adds stress to the product side of the reduction half-equation.
2. **Equilibrium Shift:** To counteract this change, the equilibrium shifts to the **left** (favouring the backward/oxidation reaction).
3. **Effect on Electrode Potential:** Because the backward reaction is favoured, the electrode potential ($E$) of the $Cl_2/Cl^-$ half-cell **decreases** (it becomes less positive than its standard $+1.36\text{ V}$).
4. **Outcome:** The $E$ value for chlorine drops below that of water. $Cl^-$ becomes **easier to oxidise** than water. 
5. **Observation:** $Cl^-$ is preferentially discharged, and the product at the anode is **Chlorine gas ($Cl_2$)**.

---

> [!summary] TL;DR: Concentration Effect on NaCl Electrolysis
> - **Dilute NaCl:** $E(Cl_2/Cl^-) > E(O_2/H_2O)$ $\rightarrow$ Water oxidised $\rightarrow$ **$O_2$ produced**.
> - **Conc NaCl:** High $[Cl^-]$ shifts $Cl_2 + 2e^- \rightleftharpoons 2Cl^-$ left $\rightarrow$ $E$ decreases $\rightarrow$ $Cl^-$ oxidised $\rightarrow$ **$Cl_2$ produced**.
