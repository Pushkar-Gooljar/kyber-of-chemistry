# 1. Subatomic Particles
- Atoms are mostly empty space surrounding a very small, dense nucleus that contains protons and neutrons.
- Electrons are found in shells in the empty space around the nucleus

## Properties of Subatomic Particles

|       Particle       |         Relative Mass         | Relative Charge | Location |
| :------------------: | :---------------------------: | :-------------: | :------: |
|   **Proton ($p$)**   |              $1$              |      $+1$       | Nucleus  |
|  **Neutron ($n$)**   |              $1$              |       $0$       | Nucleus  |
| **Electron ($e^-$)** | $\frac{1}{1836}$ (negligible) |      $-1$       |  Shells  |

---
# 2. Definitions and Notation

**Standard Nuclide Notation:**
$$
\Large
\ce{^{A}_{Z}X}
$$
- **Atomic Number ($Z$):** The number of **protons** in the nucleus. 
- **Mass Number ($A$):** The total number of **protons + neutrons** in the nucleus.

>[!example] Calculation Example: Ion $\ce{^{79}Se^{2+}}$
>- **Protons:** 34 (from periodic table).
>- **Neutrons:** $79 - 34  = 45$.
>- **Electrons:** $34+2=36$ (Anion has gained electrons).

# 3. Behaviour in Electric Fields
When beams of subatomic particles move at the same velocity through an electric field, their deflection depends on their **Charge-to-Mass ratio ($\frac{q}{m}$)**.

$$
\large
\text{Angle of Deflection} \propto \frac{\text{charge}}{\text{mass}}
$$

## Deflection Summary

|   Beam    | Direction of Deflection | Magnitude of Deflection |                    Reason                     |
| :-------: | :---------------------: | :---------------------: | :-------------------------------------------: |
|  Protons  | Towards negative plate  |          Small          |         High mass ($1$), Charge $+1$          |
| Electrons | Towards positive plate  |     **Very Large**      | Very low mass ($\frac{1}{1836}$), Charge $-1$ |
| Neutrons  |      No deflection      |          None           |                 Charge is $0$                 |

# 4. Atomic and Ionic Radius Trends
>[!info] Atomic Radius
>It is **half** the distance between the two **nuclei** of two covalently bonded atoms of the same type.
>
>![The atomic radius of a hydrogen atom|400](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/cdn-cgi/image/f=auto,width=1080/https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/1.1-Atomic-Structure-Atomic-Radius-Hydrogen-Molecule.png)

</br>

## A. Atomic Radius Across a period (e.g. Na $\to$ Cl)  


$$
\large 
\begin{align} \\
\text{Across a period atomic radius decreases.}\\ \\

\end{align}
$$


>[!important] Explanation
>Across a period:
>1. The **nuclear charge** **increases**.
>2. Electrons are added to the **same outer shell**.
>3. **Shielding is relatively unchanged**.
>4. There is a **greater attraction** between the nucleus and the outer electrons, pulling the electrons closer to the nucleus.


## B. Atomic Radius Down a group


$$
\large 
\begin{align} \\
\text{Down a group atomic radius increases.}\\ \\

\end{align}
$$


>[!important] Explanation
>Down a group:
>1. The number of **electron shells increases**.
>2. The distance between the nucleus and outer electrons increases.
>3. **Shielding increases**.
>4. The nuclear attractions for outer electrons decreases.

## C. Ionic Radius: Cation vs Parent Atom (e.g. $\ce{Mg}$ vs $\ce{Mg^2+}$)



$$
\large 
\begin{align} \\
\text{Cations are \textbf{smaller} than their parent atoms.}\\ \\

\end{align}
$$



>[!important] Explanation
>1. The atom loses its outer shell electrons (one fewer shell).
>2. There is **less electron-electron repulsion**.
>3. The remaining electrons are attracted by the **same nuclear charge** and are pulled closer to the nucleus



## D. Ionic Radius: Anion vs Parent Atom (e.g. $\ce{Cl}$ vs $\ce{Cl-}$)



$$
\large 
\begin{align} \\
\text{Anions are \textbf{larger} than their parent atoms.}\\ \\

\end{align}
$$



>[!important] Explanation
>1. The atom gains electrons into the same outer shell.
>2. This causes **increased electron-electron repulsion**.
>3. The **same nuclear charge** must hold more electrons, so the electrons move further apart.

## E. Ionic Radius Trend Across a Period (The "Step Change")
Ionic radius does **not** decrease continuously across a period. There is a distinct "jump" between the group 14 and 15 ions (e.g., Period 3).

$$
\large
\begin{align} \\
\text{Trend: Cations Decrease } \to \textbf{ MASSIVE JUMP } \to \text{Anions Decrease} \\ \\
\end{align}
$$

**Visualizing Period 3:**
1.  **Cations ($\ce{Na+}$ to $\ce{Si^4+}$):** Radius **decreases** (Isoelectronic with $\ce{Ne}$).
2.  **The Jump ($\ce{Si^4+}$ to $\ce{P^3-}$):** Radius **increases dramatically**.
3.  **Anions ($\ce{P^3-}$ to $\ce{Cl-}$):** Radius **decreases** (Isoelectronic with $\ce{Ar}$).

>[!important] Explanation of the Jump
>Why is $\ce{P^3-}$ much larger than $\ce{Si^4+}$?
>1. **Shells:** The cations have lost their outer shell (occupying $n=2$). The anions have gained electrons to fill the next shell ($n=3$).
>2. **Shielding:** The anions have an **extra electron shell**, significantly increasing shielding and distance from the nucleus compared to the cations.

# 5. Isoelectronic Series
**Isoelectronic species** have the same number of electrons and the same electronic configuration.
*Example:* $\text{N}^{3-}, \text{O}^{2-}, \text{F}^{-}, \text{Na}^{+}, \text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Al}^{3+}$ (all have 10 electrons: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6$).




$$
\large 
\begin{align} \\
\text{Ionic radius \textbf{decreases} as atomic number increases}\\ \\

\end{align}
$$



>[!important] Explanation: Why is $\ce{Al^3+}$ smaller than $\ce{Mg^2+}$
>1. They have the **same number of electrons** and same shielding.
>2. $\ce{Al^3+}$ has a **greater nuclear charge** than $\ce{Mg^2+}$.
>3. There is a **greater nuclear attraction** for the remaining electrons in $\ce{Al^3+}$, pulling them closer to the nucleus.
