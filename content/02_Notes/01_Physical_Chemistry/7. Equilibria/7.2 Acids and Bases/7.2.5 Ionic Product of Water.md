# The Auto-Ionisation of Water
Water is not just a passive solvent; it is an active participant in acid-base chemistry. Even completely pure water conducts electricity slightly because it undergoes **auto-ionisation**.

Water molecules collide and transfer protons to one another, acting as both an **acid** and a **base** (amphoteric).
$$
\large
\ce{H_{2}O_{(l)} + H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons H_{3}O+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)}}
$$
Or, simplified:
$$
\large
\ce{H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)}}
$$
>[!info]
>The position of equilibrium lies extremely far to the left. Only about 1 in every 500 million water molecules is ionised at room temperature.

---
# Deriving $\ce{K_{w}}$ from $\ce{K_{c}}$
If we write the Equilibrium Constant expression ($\ce{K_{c}}$) for the simplified dissociation:
$$
\large
\mathrm{K_{c}} = \frac{\ce{[H+][OH-]}}{[\ce{H_{2}O}]}
$$
Since the dissociation is so small, the concentration of water is effectively constant.

We multiply $\ce{K_{c}}$ by this constant to get a new constant: $\ce{K_{w}}$
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\ce{K_{c}} &= \frac{\ce{[H+][OH-]}}{\ce{[H_{2}O]}} \quad (\times \ce{[H_{2}O]}) \\ \\
\ce{K_{c}} \times \ce{[H_{2}O]} &= \ce{[H+][OH-]} \\ \\
\ce{K_{w}} &= \ce{[H+][OH-]}

\end{align}
$$

>[!tip]- Insight: Why is $\ce{[H_{2}O]}$ taken as constant?
>**What does $[\ce{H_{2}O}]$ actually mean?**
>- Square brackets $[ \space]$ mean "concentration in $\ce{moldm^{-3}}$"
>- So, $\ce{[H_{2}O]}$ literally asks: **"How many moles of water molecules are there in one cubic decimetre of water?"**
>
>Let's calculate it for pure water at $\mathrm{25°C}$:
>1. **Mass of $\mathrm{1 \space dm^{3}}$ water:** Density is $\mathrm{1000 \space gdm^{-3}}$, so mass is $\mathrm{1000g}$.
>2. **Molar Mass of water ($\ce{Mr}$):** $\mathrm{18.0 \space gmol^{-1}}$
>3. **Number of Moles $\ce{H_{2}O}$:**
>$$\large\mathrm{\text{Moles}=\frac{\text{Mass}}{Mr}=\frac{1000}{18.0}=55.6 \space mol}$$
>
>So, the concentration of pure water is a massive $\mathrm{55.6 \space moldm^{-3}}$
>
>**Why is it taken to be constant?**
>The equilibrium for water dissociation is:
>$$\large
\ce{H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)}}$$
>We know that water dissociates extremely weekly.
>At equilibrium, the concentration of ions formed is only $\mathrm{1.0 \times 10^{-7} \space moldm^{-3}}$. (from conductivity experiments)
>
>Let's compare the Initial concentration vs. the Equilibrium concentration:
>- **Initial $\ce{[H_{2}O]}:$** $55.555555\dots$
>- **Change:** $-0.0000001$
>- **Final $\ce{[H_{2}O]}:$** $55.5555549\dots$
>
>**The Conclusion:**
>The value has changed by such an infinitesimally small amount that mathematically, it is considered **unchanged.**

# Definition of $\ce{K_{w}}$
$\ce{K_{w}}$ is the **Ionic Product of Water.**
$$
\large
\ce{K_{w} = [H+][OH-]}
$$
- **Standard Value at 298K:**
$$
\large
\ce{K_{w}} = 1.00 \times 10^{-14} \space \mathrm{mol^2dm^{-6}}
$$
>[!info]
>- From conductivity experiments, it has been found that the concentration of ions in pure water at 298K is $1.00 \times 10^{-7} \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$
>- This means that $1 \space \mathrm{dm^3}$ of pure water at room temperature contains:
>	- $1.00 \times 10^{-7} \space \mathrm{mol}$  $\ce{H+}$
>	- $1.00 \times 10^{-7} \space \mathrm{mol}$  $\ce{OH-}$
>- Applying this to $\ce{K_{w}} = \ce{[H+][OH-]}$ we get that:
>$$\large \begin{align} \ce{K_{w}} &= 1.00 \times 10^{-7}\space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}} \times 1.00 \times 10^{-7}\space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}} \\ \ce{K_{w}} &= 1.00 \times 10^{-14}\space \mathrm{mol^2dm^{-6}} \end{align}$$

>[!info]
>In any aqueous system, water behaves in such a way that:
>$$\large \ce{[H+][OH-]} = 1.00 \times 10^{-14}$$

- **Temperature Dependence:** Like all equilibrium constants, $\ce{K_{w}}$ changes with temperature.
	- The ionisation of water is **endothermic** (bond breaking).
	- As temperature **increases**, the equilibrium shifts to the right.
	- Therefore, $\ce{K_{w}}$ **increases** as water gets hotter.

# The Definition of Neutrality 
This is a critical conceptual point: **"Neutral" does not mean pH 7**:
- **Neutral** means:
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]}=\ce{[OH-]}
$$
- **Acidic** means:
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]}>\ce{[OH-]}
$$
- **Alkaline** means:
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]}<\ce{[OH-]}
$$
## Why is Pure Water pH 7 at 25°C?
In pure water, for every $\ce{H+}$ formed, one $\ce{OH-}$ is formed, therefore:
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]} = \ce{[OH-]}
$$
Substitute this into the $\ce{K_{w}}$ expression:
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\ce{K_{w}} &= \ce{[H+][OH-]} \\
\ce{K_{w}} &= \ce{[H+]}^2 \\
1.00 \times 10^{-14} &= \ce{[H+]}^2 \\
\ce{[H+]} &= \sqrt{ 1.00 \times 10^{-14} } \\
&=1.00 \times 10^{-7} \\ \\ \\
\mathrm{pH} &= -\log(\ce{[H+]}) \\
&= -\log(1.00 \times 10^{-7}) \\
&= 7.00
\end{align}
$$
>[!danger] Critical: Non room-temperature water
>- At higher temperatures (e.g. 100°C), $\ce{K_{w}}$ is larger (e.g. $5.00 \times 10^{-13}$).
>- The pH of boiling water is roughly **6.1**.
>- Does this mean boiling water is acidic? ***ABSOLUTELY NOT***
>- It is still **neutral** because $\ce{[H+]} = \ce{[OH-]}$.
>- There is simply a higher concentration of both ions.
>  
>  (Same reasoning for cold water not being more alkaline)

# Using $\ce{K_{w}}$ for Strong Alkalis
- $\ce{K_{w}}$ is the key to calculating the pH of strong bases like $\ce{NaOH}$.
- Since $\ce{NaOH}$ fully dissociates, we know $\ce{[OH-]}$, but the PH equation requires $\ce{[H+]}$.

**The Relationship:**
In **any** aqueous solution (acid, base, or neutral), the product of the ions must always equal $\ce{K_{w}}$.
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\ce{K_{w}} &= \ce{[H+][OH-]} \\
\ce{[H+] &= \ce{\frac{K_{w}}{[OH-]}}}
\end{align}
$$
