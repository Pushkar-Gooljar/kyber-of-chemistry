## What is a Buffer?
>[!info] Definition: Buffer
>A buffer solution is a solution that **resists changes** in pH when **small amounts** of acid or alkali are added.

>[!tip] Key Terminology
>- **"Resists Change":** It does **NOT** keep pH constant. The pH changes ***slightly***, but drastically less than if the buffer was not there.
>- **"Small amounts":** Every buffer has a "limit". If you dump too much acid/alkali in the buffer, it will be overwhelmed.


---
## Types of Buffers
There are two main ways to make a buffer solution, depending on whether you want to stabilise an acidic pH or an alkaline pH.
### A. Acidic Buffer (pH < 7)
Made from a **Weak Acid** and its **Conjugate Base** (Salt).
- **Recipe:** Ethanoic Acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) + Sodium Ethanoate ($\ce{CH_{3}COO- Na+}$).

### B. Basic Buffer (pH > 7 )
Made from a **Weak Base**and its **Conjugate Acid** (Salt).
- **Recipe:** Ammonia ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) + Ammonium Chloride ($\ce{NH_{4}Cl}$).

---
## Acidic Buffer Mechanism 
### Setup
Consider a buffer containing a high concentration of Ethanoic Acid ($\ce{HA}$) and Ethanoate ions ($\ce{A-}$):

- Ethanoic acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) is a **Weak Acid** and partially ionises in solution to form a relatively **low** concentration of ethanoate ions ($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$).

$$
\large

\ce{CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)}}
$$

</br>

- Sodium Ethanoate ($\ce{CH_{3}COO- Na+}$) is a **salt** which fully ionises in solution to form a relatively **high** concentration of ethanoate ions ($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$).

$$
\large
\ce{CH_{3}COONa_{(aq)} -> CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)} + Na+_{(aq)}}
$$

</br>

These form the **reserve supplies** of the acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) and its conjugate base
($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$). 

A buffer solution contains:
- Relatively high concentrations of acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$), due to the partial ionisation of the weak acid.
- Relatively high concentrations of the acid's conjugate base ($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$), due to the complete ionisation of the salt ($\ce{CH_{3}COONa}$).


This leads to an equilibrium between the acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) and its conjugate base
($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$):
$$
\large

\ce{CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)}}
$$
### Scenario 1: Adding Acid ($\ce{H+}$)
- **Disturbance:** $\ce{HCl}$ is added to the buffer solution, concentration of $\ce{H+}$ rises.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the left.
	- The excess $\ce{H+}$ ions react with the $\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$ to form $\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$ until equilibrium is re-established.

$$
\large
\ce{
H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)} -> CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)}
}
$$
- **Result:** The added $\ce{H+}$ is consumed and turned into weak acid. The pH **drops only very slightly**.

### Scenario 2: Adding Alkali ($\ce{OH-}$)
- **Disturbance:** $\ce{NaOH}$ is added to the buffer solution, the $\ce{OH-}$ reacts with the $\ce{H+}$ to form water, concentration of $\ce{H+}$ drops.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the right.
	- The acid ionises to form more $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$ until equilibrium is re-established.

$$
\large
\ce{
CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)} -> H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)}}
$$

- As there is a large supply of $\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$, the change in concentration of $\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$  is negligible when it dissociates to form more $\ce{H+}$ ions.
</br>
- As there is a large supply of $\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$, the change in concentration of $\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$ is negligible when more $\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$ is formed by the dissociation of $\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$.
</br>
- **Result:** The $\ce{H+}$ concentration is restored. The pH **rises only very slightly.**

>[!warning]- Warning: pH does not remain the same
>- When a strong acid or alkali is added to a buffer solution, the pH does not come back exactly the same as it was before.
>- This is because a buffer solution does not **stop** changes in pH, it **resists**.
>
>**Scenario 1: $\ce{H+}$ is added**
>- Position of equilibrium shifts to the left:
>$$\large \ce{H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)} -> CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)}}$$
>- Concentration weak acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) increases.
>- Concentration of conjugate base ($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$) decreases.
>- According to the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:
>$$\large \mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} + \log\left(\frac{\mathrm{[A^-]}}{\mathrm{[HA]}}\right)$$
>	- $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ remains constant for a given weak acid.
>	- $\mathrm{[A^-]}$ decreases.
>	- $\mathrm{[HA]}$ increases.
>	- The logarithmic term will be smaller than it previously was, as the ratio $\frac{\mathrm{[A^-]}}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$ becomes smaller.
>	- Therefore, the pH will slightly decrease.
>
>**Scenario 2: $\ce{OH-}$ is added**
>- Position of equilibrium shifts to the right:
>$$\large \ce{CH_{3}COOH_{(aq)} -> H+_{(aq)} + CH_{3}COO-_{(aq)}}$$
>- Concentration weak acid ($\ce{CH_{3}COOH}$) decreases.
>- Concentration of conjugate base ($\ce{CH_{3}COO-}$) increases.
>- According to the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:
>$$\large \mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} + \log\left(\frac{\mathrm{[A^-]}}{\mathrm{[HA]}}\right)$$
>	- $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ remains constant for a given weak acid.
>	- $\mathrm{[A^-]}$ increases.
>	- $\mathrm{[HA]}$ decreases.
>	- The logarithmic term will be larger than it previously was, as the ratio $\frac{\mathrm{[A^-]}}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$ becomes larger.
>	- Therefore, the pH will slightly increase.

## Alkaline Buffer Mechanism 

### Setup
Consider a buffer containing a high concentration of Ammonia ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) and ammonium ions 
($\ce{NH_{4}+}$):
- Ammonia is a **Weak Base** and partially ionises in solution to form a relatively **low** concentration of ammonium ions ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$).

$$
\large
\ce{
NH_{3}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons NH_{4}+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)}
}
$$
- Ammonium chloride ($\ce{NH_{4}Cl}$) is a **salt** which fully ionises in solution to form a relatively **high** concentration of ammonium ions ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$).

$$
\large
\ce{
NH_{4}Cl_{(aq)} -> NH_{4}+_{(aq)} + Cl-_{(aq)}
}
$$

These form the **reserve supplies** of the base ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) and its conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$).

A buffer solution contains:
- Relatively high concentrations of base ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) due to the partial ionisation of the base.
- Relatively high concentrations of the base's conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$), due to the complete ionisation of the salt ($\ce{NH_{4}Cl}$).

This leads to an equilibrium between the base ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) and its conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$):

$$
\large
\ce{
NH_{3}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons NH_{4}+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)}
}
$$
### Scenario 1: Adding Acid ($\ce{H+}$)
- **Disturbance:** $\ce{HCl}$ is added to the buffer solution, the concentration of $\ce{H+}$ rises.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the right.
	- The excess $\ce{H+}$ ions react with the $\ce{NH_{3}}$ to form $\ce{NH_{4}+}$ until equilibrium is re-established.

$$
\large
\ce{
H+_{(aq)} + NH_{3}_{(aq)} -> NH_{4}+_{(aq)}
}
$$
- **Result:** The added $\ce{H+}$ is consumed and turned into the conjugate acid of the weak base ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$). The pH **drops only very slightly**.

>[!tip] Why doesn't the $\ce{H+}$ react with $\ce{OH-}$ instead?
>Some of the $\ce{H+}$ does react with the $\ce{OH-}$, however:
>- The concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ is negligible compared to concentration of $\ce{NH_{3}}$.
>	- For example, while the concentration of $\ce{NH_{3}}$ is usually around 0.1 to 1.0 M, at a pH of 9 (weak alkali), the pOH is 5, meaning the concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ is about $0.00001$ M.
>- It is statistically more likely for the $\ce{H+}$ to find a $\ce{NH_{3}}$ molecule to react with than a $\ce{OH-}$.
>  - When some of the $\ce{OH-}$ do react with the $\ce{H+}$, the equilibrium shifts to replace them by the dissociation of $\ce{NH_{3}}$.




### Scenario 2: Adding Alkali ($\ce{OH-}$)
- **Disturbance:** $\ce{NaOH}$ is added to the buffer solution. The concentration of $\ce{OH-}$ rises.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the left.
	- The conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}+}$) acts as an acid, it donates a proton ($\ce{H+}$) to the incoming $\ce{OH-}$, turning it into water.
$$
\large
\ce{
NH_{4}+_{(aq)} + OH-_{(aq)} -> NH_{3}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)}
}
$$
- **Result:** The added $\ce{OH-}$ is turned into water. The pH **rises only very slightly**.

>[!warning]- Warning: pH does not remain the same
>- When a strong acid or alkali is added to a buffer solution, the pH does not come back exactly the same as it was before.
>- This is because a buffer solution does not **stop** changes in pH, it **resists**.
>
>**Scenario 1: $\ce{H+}$ is added**
>- The weak base reacts with the protons:
>$$\large \ce{H+_{(aq)} + NH_{3}_{(aq)} -> NH_{4}^{+}_{(aq)}}$$
>- Concentration of conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}^{+}}$) increases.
>- Concentration of weak base ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) decreases.
>- According to the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:
>$$\large \mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} + \log\left(\frac{\mathrm{[Base]}}{\mathrm{[Acid]}}\right)$$
>	- $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ remains constant for the ammonium ion.
>	- $\mathrm{[Base]}$ ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) decreases.
>	- $\mathrm{[Acid]}$ ($\ce{NH_{4}^{+}}$) increases.
>	- The logarithmic term will be smaller than it previously was, as the ratio $\frac{\mathrm{[Base]}}{[\mathrm{Acid}]}$ becomes smaller.
>	- Therefore, the pH will slightly decrease.
>
>**Scenario 2: $\ce{OH-}$ is added**
>- The conjugate acid neutralizes the hydroxide:
>$$\large \ce{NH_{4}^{+}_{(aq)} + OH^{-}_{(aq)} -> NH_{3}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)}}$$
>- Concentration of conjugate acid ($\ce{NH_{4}^{+}}$) decreases.
>- Concentration of weak base ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) increases.
>- According to the Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation:
>$$\large \mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} + \log\left(\frac{\mathrm{[Base]}}{\mathrm{[Acid]}}\right)$$
>	- $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ remains constant for the ammonium ion.
>	- $\mathrm{[Base]}$ ($\ce{NH_{3}}$) increases.
>	- $\mathrm{[Acid]}$ ($\ce{NH_{4}^{+}}$) decreases.
>	- The logarithmic term will be larger than it previously was, as the ratio $\frac{\mathrm{[Base]}}{[\mathrm{Acid}]}$ becomes larger.
>	- Therefore, the pH will slightly increase.

## Use of Buffers: Blood
### Context
The pH of human blood plasma must be tightly maintained between **7.35 and 7.45**.
- If pH < 7.35: **Acidosis** (leads to fatigue, shortness of breath, shock).
- If pH > 7.45: **Alkalosis** (leads to muscle spasms, nausea).

To survive, the body uses the **Hydrogencarbonate ion** ($\ce{HCO_{3}-}$) as the conjugate base and **Carbonic Acid** ($\ce{H_{2}CO_{3}}$) as the weak acid.

>[!warning]- Warning: Blood uses an acidic buffer system and not alkaline.
>- Given that blood pH need to be between 7.35 and 7.45, which are both above 7, you would assume that an alkaline buffer would be needed.
>- However an important distinction needs to be made:
>  
>  
><p style="text-align:center"><b>Acidic buffer does not always mean pH less than 7</b></p>
>
>- An **acidic buffer** is simply a buffer made from a **Weak Acid + Conjugate Base**.
>- It can buffer **any pH** depending on the $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ of the acid and the **Ratio of $\mathrm{\frac{[Base]}{[Acid]}}$**.
>
>**The Mathematical Proof (Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation)**
>- The pH of a buffer is determined by:
>$$\large\mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} + \log\left(\mathrm{\frac{[Base]}{[Acid]}}\right)$$
>
>For the blood buffer (Carbonic Acid):
>- $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$: The $p\mathrm{K_{a}}$ of Carbonic Acid ($\ce{H_{2}CO_{3}}$) is roughly **6.1**.
>- **If \[Base] = \[Acid]:** $\log(1) = 0$, so $\mathrm{pH} = p\mathrm{K_{a}} = 6.1$ (Too acidic for life!)
>  
>  How does the blood pH get up to 7.4?
>- The body maintains a massive imbalance in the acid and conjugate base concentrations.
>- It keeps the concentration of **Bicarbonate** (base) roughly **20 times higher** than Carbonic Acid.
>$$\large\begin{aligned}\mathrm{pH}&=6.1 + \log(20) \\\mathrm{pH} &= 6.1 + 1.3 \\ \mathrm{pH}&= \mathbf{7.4}\end{aligned}$$
>
>**Conclusion**
>An acidic buffer system can maintain an alkaline pH (7.4) simply by having a **huge excess of the conjugate base** relative to the weak acid.


### Formation of the Buffer Mixture
The buffer components are naturally generated from cellular respiration.
1. **Respiration:** Cells produce $\ce{CO_{2}}$ as a waste product.
2. **Dissolution:** $\ce{CO_{2}}$ dissolves in blood plasma and reacts with water.
	- This reaction is catalysed by the enzyme *Carbonic Anhydrase*

$$
\large
\ce{
CO_{2}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons H_{2}CO_{3}_{(aq)}
}
$$
3. **Dissociation:** The carbonic acid is a weak acid, so it partially dissociates to establish the buffer equilibrium.
$$
\large
\ce{
H_{2}CO_{3}_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + HCO_{3}-_{(aq)}
}
$$

>[!tip] The "Effective" Equilibrium
>Since $\ce{H_{2}CO_{3}}$ is unstable, the combined equilibrium equation is often preferred:
>$$\large \ce{CO_{2}_{(aq)} + H_{2}O_{(l)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + HCO_{3}-_{(aq)}}$$

### Regulation of Blood pH
The system relies on the large reservoir of **$\text{HCO}_3^-$** (to soak up acid) and the ability of the lungs to remove **$\text{CO}_2$** (to remove acid).

#### Scenario 1: Excess Acid (e.g. Lactic Acid from exercise)
- **Disturbance:** Concentration of $\ce{H+}$ in blood rises.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the left.
	- The conjugate base accepts protons:
$$\large \ce{HCO3^{-}_{(aq)} + H^{+}_{(aq)} \rightarrow H2CO3_{(aq)} \rightarrow CO2_{(aq)} + H2O_{(l)}} $$
- **Regulation:** The excess $\text{CO}_2$ produced is transported to the lungs and exhaled. This prevents the acid form ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$) from building up and shifting the equilibrium back.

#### Scenario 2: Excess Alkali (rare, e.g. ingesting alkaline drugs)
- **Disturbance:** Excess $\ce{OH-}$ reacts with $\ce{H+}$ to form water, concentration of $\ce{H+}$ in blood drops.
- **Response:** Position of equilibrium shifts to the right.
	- Carbonic acid dissociates to restore the lost $\ce{H+}$.
$$\large \ce{H2CO3_{(aq)} \rightarrow H^{+}_{(aq)} + HCO3^{-}_{(aq)}} $$
- **Regulation:** The kidneys help remove the excess $\text{HCO}_3^-$ in urine if necessary.
