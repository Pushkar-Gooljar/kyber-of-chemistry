# Understanding p-notation 
In chemistry, the lowercase letter "**p**" is a mathematical operator. It is shorthand for "**negative logarithm to the base of 10**":
$$
\large
\mathrm{pX} = -\log_{10}(\mathrm{X})
$$
**p-notation** is used to convert very small, unmanageable numbers (e.g. $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$) into convenient, manageable numbers (e.g. $4.89$).

**Examples uses of p-notation:**

|  Term   |         Meaning         |      Conversion      |
| :-----: | :---------------------: | :------------------: |
| **pH**  |    Power of Hydrogen    |  $-\lg([\ce{H+}])$   |
| **pOH** |   Power of Hydroxide    |  $-\lg([\ce{OH-}])$  |
| **pKa** | Power of Acid Constant  | $-\lg([\ce{K_{a}}])$ |
| **pKw** | Power of Water Constant | $-\lg([\ce{K_{w}}])$ |
>[!info] The "Inverse" Rule
>Because of the **negative** sign in the logarithm:
>- **High p-value** = Low actual concentration/constant.
>- **Low p-value** = High actual concentration/constant.
>
>*Example*: A **low pH** means **high $[\ce{H+}]$**

---
# Definition of pH
**pH** is a logarithmic scale to express the concentration of hydrogen ions ($\ce{[H+]}$).
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\mathrm{pH} &= -\log_{10}[\ce{H+}] \\ \\
\ce{[H+]} &= 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}
\end{align}
$$
>[!info] Significant Figures Rule
>The number of decimal places in the pH value corresponds to the number of significant figures in the concentration.
>**Example:**
>$$\large  \begin{align} \ce{[H+]} &= 0.0010 \quad (\text{2 significant figures}) \\[4pt] \mathrm{pH} &= -\log_{10}(0.0010) \\[4pt] &= 3.00 \quad (\text{2 decimal places}) \end{align}$$

---
# Strong Acids
A strong acid **fully dissociates**. Therefore, the concentration of $\ce{H+}$ is derived directly from the concentration of the acid.

## Case A: Monoprotic Acids ($\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{HNO_{3}}$)
A monoprotic acid in the form $\ce{HA}$ **fully dissociates** as follows:
$$
\large
\ce{HA -> H+ + A-}
$$

Ratio of **acid** to **$\ce{H+}$** is $1:1$,
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]} = [\text{Acid}]
$$
</br>

>[!example] Example: Find the pH of $0.05 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$ $\ce{HNO_{3}}$
>$$\large \begin{align} \ce{[H+]} &= \ce{[HNO_{3}]}  \\[4pt]\ce{[H+]} &= 0.05 \\[4pt] pH &= -\log(0.05) \\[4pt]&= 1.3010\\[4pt] &= 1.30 \end{align}$$


## Case B: Diprotic Acids ($\ce{H_{2}SO_{4}}$)
A diprotic acid in the form $\ce{H_{2}A}$ **fully dissociates** as follows:
$$
\large
\ce{H_{2}A -> 2H+ + A-}
$$

Ratio of **acid** to **$\ce{H+}$** is $1:2$,
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]} = 2 \times[\text{Acid}]
$$
</br>

>[!example] Example: Find the pH of $0.05 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$ $\ce{H_{2}SO_{4}}$
>$$\large \begin{align} \ce{[H+]} &= 2 \times \ce{[H_{2}SO_{4}]}  \\[4pt]\ce{[H+]} &= 2 \times 0.05 \\[4pt] &= 0.10 \\[4pt] pH &= -\log(0.10) \\[4pt]&= 1.00 \end{align}$$

# Strong Bases (Alkalis)
- A strong base **fully dissociates** to release $\ce{OH-}$ **not** $\ce{H+}$. 
- We cannot calculate pH directly from the base concentration. 
- We must use the **Ionic Product of Water** ($\ce{K_{w}}$) to find $\ce{[H+]}$ first.

**To find $\ce{[H+]}$ from $\ce{[OH-]}$:**
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\ce{K_{w}} &= \ce{[H+][OH-]} \\[4pt]
\ce{[H+]} &= \frac{\ce{K_{w}}}{\ce{[OH-]}}
\end{align}
$$
*(Use $\ce{K_{w}}=1.00 \times 10^{-14}$ unless told otherwise.)*

## The 3-Step Method
1. **Find $\ce{[OH-]}$:** Look at the concentration of the alkali and use the appropriate mol ratio for $\text{alkali}:\ce{[OH-]}$ (e.g. $1:1$ for $\ce{NaOH}$ or $1:2$ for $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$).
2. **Find $\ce{H+}$:** 
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{\ce{[OH-]}}
$$
3. **Calculate pH:**
$$
\large
\mathrm{pH}=-\log[\ce{H+}]
$$
### Example 1: Group 1 Alkali ($\ce{NaOH}$)
>[!example] Find the pH of $0.10 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$ $\ce{NaOH}$
>1. **Find $\ce{[OH-]}$**
>$$\large\ce{NaOH -> Na+ + OH-}$$
>Ratio of $\ce{NaOH}:\ce{OH-}$ is $1:1$
>$$\large \begin{align} [\ce{OH-}] &= [\ce{NaOH}]\\[4pt] [\ce{OH-}] &= 0.10\end{align}$$
>2. **Find $\ce{[H+]}$**
>$$\large \begin{align} [\ce{H+}] &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{[\ce{OH-}]} \\[4pt] [\ce{H+}] &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{0.10} \\[4pt] &= 1.0 \times 10^{-13}\end{align}$$
>3. **Find pH**
>$$\large \begin{align} \mathrm{pH} &= -\log\ce{[H+]}\\[4pt] \mathrm{pH}&=-\log(1.0 \times 10^{-13}) \\[4pt] &= 13.00\end{align}$$

### Example 2: Group 2 Alkali ($\ce{Ba(OH)2}$)
>[!example] Find the pH of $0.05 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$ $\ce{Ba(OH)2}$
>1. **Find $\ce{[OH-]}$**
>$$\large\ce{Ba(OH)2 -> Ba^2+ + 2OH-}$$
>Ratio of $\ce{Ba(OH)2}:\ce{OH-}$ is $1:2$
>$$\large \begin{align} [\ce{OH-}] &= 2 \times[\ce{Ba(OH)2}]\\[4pt] [\ce{OH-}] &= 2 \times 0.05 \\[4pt] &= 0.10\end{align}$$
>2. **Find $\ce{[H+]}$**
>$$\large \begin{align} [\ce{H+}] &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{[\ce{OH-}]} \\[4pt] [\ce{H+}] &= \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{0.10} \\[4pt] &= 1.0 \times 10^{-13}\end{align}$$
>3. **Find pH**
>$$\large \begin{align} \mathrm{pH} &= -\log\ce{[H+]}\\[4pt] \mathrm{pH}&=-\log(1.0 \times 10^{-13}) \\[4pt] &= 13.00\end{align}$$

> [!tip] Alternative Method: pOH
> Some students prefer calculating pOH first.
> $$ \text{pOH} = -\log[OH^-] $$
> $$ \text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14 $$
> Both methods are fully accepted in exams.

>[!info] Proof: $\mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} = 14$
>$$\large \begin{align}\mathrm{pH}&=-\log[\ce{H+}]\\ \mathrm{pOH}&=-\log[\ce{OH-}] \\ \\ \mathrm{pH} + \mathrm{pOH} &= -\log[\ce{H+}] -\log[\ce{OH-}]\\ &= -(\log[\ce{H+}] +\log[\ce{OH-}]) \\ &= -\log[\ce{H+}][\ce{OH-}] \\ &= -\log(\ce{K_{w}}) \\ &= -\log(1.00 \times 10^{-14}) \\ &= 14\end{align}$$
