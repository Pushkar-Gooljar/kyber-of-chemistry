# The Acid Dissociation Constant ($\ce{K_{a}}$)
Since weak acids only **partially dissociate**, we cannot just say
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]}= \ce{[\text{Acid}]}
$$
We must use an equilibrium constant to calculate the equilibrium concentration of $\ce{[H+]}$.

For a weak acid $\ce{HA}$:
$$
\large
\ce{HA_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons H+_{(aq)} + A-_{(aq)}}
$$
The expression for the Acid Dissociation Constant is:
$$
\large
\ce{K_{a}}= \frac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{A-}]}{\ce{[HA]}}
$$
>[!info] Units of $\ce{K_{a}}$
>For the use case of this syllabus, it can be said that $\ce{K_{a}}$ always has units of $\mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$.

>[!tip] How $\ce{K_{a}}$ relates to acid strength 
>- **Large value of $\ce{K_{a}}$**
>	- Equilibrium lies to the right (stronger acid)
>- **Small value of $\ce{K_{a}}$**
>	- Equilibrium lies to the left (weaker acid)

---
# $\ce{K_{a}}$ vs $\ce{pK_{a}}$
- Just like $\ce{[H+]}$, $\ce{K_{a}}$ values are often very small and difficult to work with (e.g. $1.7 \times 10^{-5}$).
- We convert them using [[7.2.6 Calculating pH - Strong Acids and Bases#Understanding p-notation|p-notation]] :
$$
\large
\begin{align}
\ce{pK_{a}} &= -\log_{10}(\ce{K_{a}}) \\[4pt]
\ce{K_{a}} &= 10^{-\ce{pK_{a}}}
\end{align}
$$
>[!tip] The Inverse Relationship of $\ce{pK_{a}}$ with acid strength
>- **Stronger** Weak Acids have **Higher** $\ce{K_{a}}$ and **Lower** $\ce{pK_{a}}$.
>- **Weaker** Weak Acids have **Lower** $\ce{K_{a}}$ and **Higher** $\ce{pK_{a}}$
></br>
>
>**Example:**
>
>| Acid      | $\ce{K_{a}}$         | $\ce{pK_{a}}$ | Strength |
>| --------- | -------------------- | ------------- | -------- |
>| Methanoic | $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ | $3.75$        | Stronger |
>| Ethanoic  | $1.7 \times 10^{-5}$ | $4.76$        | Weaker   |

---
# Calculating pH of Weak Acids
To calculate pH, we need to find $\ce{[H+]}$ using the $\ce{K_{a}}$ expression for the given acid's (partial) dissociation:
$$
\large
\ce{K_{a}}= \frac{[\ce{H+}][\ce{A-}]}{\ce{[HA]}}
$$
## The Two Approximations
We can simplify the $\ce{K_{a}}$ expression using two key approximations.
### 1. Ignore $\ce{H+}$ from water

>[!info] The Approximation
>$$\large [\ce{H+}] \approx \ce{[A-]}$$
>Therefore:
>$$\large [\ce{H+}][\ce{A-}] = [\ce{H+}]^2$$

**The Explanation:**
- Although pure water produces a tiny amount of $\ce{H+}$ ($10^{-7}$), the weak acid produces significantly more. 
- Therefore, we assume **all** $\ce{H+}$ ions come from the acid.

Since
$$
\large
\ce{HA \rightleftharpoons H+ + A-}
$$
produces ions in a $1:1$ ratio, if we ignore water, the concentration of $\ce{H+}$ must (approximately) equal the concentration of $\ce{A-}$:

$$
\large
[\ce{H+}] \approx [\ce{A-}]
$$
Therefore, the numerator 
$$
\large
[\ce{H+}][\ce{A-}]
$$
becomes:
$$
\large
\ce{[H+]}^2
$$
### 2. Dissociation is Negligible
>[!info] The Approximation
>$$\large [\ce{HA}]_{\text{equilibrium}} = [\ce{HA}]_{\text{initial}}$$

**The Explanation:**
- Weak acids dissociate very slightly (often < 1%).
- Therefore, the amount of acid molecules that break apart is mathematically insignificant compared to the large amount that remain intact.

$$
\large
[\ce{HA}]_{\text{equilibrium}} = [\ce{HA}]_{{\text{initial}}} - [\ce{H+}]
$$
Since $\ce{H+}$ is tiny,
$$
\large
[\ce{HA}]_{\text{initial}} - [\ce{H+}] \approx [\ce{HA}]_{\text{initial}}
$$
Therefore, instead of finding out the concentration of $\ce{HA}$ at equilibrium to use in the denominator of the $\ce{K_{a}}$ expression, you can just use the concentration of $\ce{HA}$ you started with (e.g. if the bottle you got the acid from said $0.1 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$, you use that as ${[\ce{HA}}]$ in the expression).

## The Simplified Formula
Substituting these approximations into the $\ce{K_{a}}$ expression:
$$
\large
\ce{K_{a}} = \frac{[\ce{H+}]^2}{[\ce{HA}]}
$$
Rearranging to solve for $\ce{[H+]}$:
$$
\large
\begin{align}
[\ce{H+}]^2 &= \ce{K_{a}} \times [\ce{HA}]  \\[4pt]
[\ce{H+}] &= \sqrt{ \ce{K_{a}} \times [\ce{HA}] }
\end{align}
$$
>[!example] Example: Calculating pH of ethanoic acid 
>**Calculate the pH of $0.10 \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$ ethanoic acid. ($\ce{K_{a}}= 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}$)**
>1. **Calculate $\ce{[H+]}$**
>$$\large \begin{align} \ce{K_{a}} &= \frac{[\ce{H+}]^2}{[\ce{HA}]} \\[4pt] [\ce{H+}] &= \sqrt{ \ce{K_{a}} \times [\ce{HA}] } \\[4pt] &= \sqrt{ 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.10 } \\[4pt] &= 1.30 \times 10^{-3} \space \mathrm{moldm^{-3}}\end{align}$$
>2. **Calculate pH**
>$$\large \begin{align} \mathrm{pH} &= -\log[\ce{H+}] \\ &= -\log(1.3 \times 10^{-3}) \\ &= 2.89\end{align}$$


---
# Limitations of the Approximations 
The formula
$$
\large
[\ce{H+}]= \sqrt{ \ce{K_{a}} \times [\ce{HA}] }
$$
works most of the time. However, the assumption that dissociation is negligible fails if:
1. The acid is stronger than usual ($\ce{K_{a}} > 10^{-2}$)
2. The solution is **extremely dilute**
