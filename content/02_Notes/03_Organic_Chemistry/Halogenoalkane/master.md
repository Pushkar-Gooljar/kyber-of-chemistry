*Tags: #A-Level-Chemistry #Organic #Halogenoalkanes #Mechanisms*

> [!warning] Examiner's Golden Rule
> The solvent determines the reaction! **Aqueous** conditions favor **Substitution** (forming alcohols). **Ethanolic** (anhydrous) conditions favor **Elimination** (forming alkenes). Mixing these up is the #1 reason students lose marks in Paper 2.

---

## 1. Classification of Halogenoalkanes
Depending on the carbon atom the halogen is attached to, halogenoalkanes are classified into three categories. This classification dictates the mechanism ($S_N1$ or $S_N2$) the molecule will undergo.

- **Primary (1°):** The halogen is attached to a carbon atom that is bonded to only **one** other alkyl group. (Undergoes $S_N2$).
- **Secondary (2°):** The halogen is attached to a carbon atom bonded to **two** other alkyl groups. (Undergoes a mixture of $S_N1$ and $S_N2$).
- **Tertiary (3°):** The halogen is attached to a carbon atom bonded to **three** other alkyl groups. (Undergoes $S_N1$).

## 2. Producing Halogenoalkanes (Synthesis)

### From Alkanes (Free-Radical Substitution)
- **Reagents:** Halogen (e.g., $Cl_2$ or $Br_2$).
- **Conditions:** **Ultraviolet (UV) light** (provides energy to break the halogen bond).
- **Mechanism Steps:** 
  1. *Initiation:* UV light causes **homolytic fission** of the halogen bond, producing two free radicals.
  2. *Propagation:* Chain reaction where radicals react with alkanes, producing new radicals.
  3. *Termination:* Two free radicals combine to form a stable molecule.
- **Examiner Note:** This method is poor for synthesizing a specific halogenoalkane because it produces a **mixture** of multiply-substituted products.

### From Alkenes (Electrophilic Addition)
- **Reagents:** Hydrogen halide ($HX$) or Halogen ($X_2$).
- **Conditions:** **Room temperature**.
- **Rule for Asymmetrical Alkenes:** Apply **Markovnikov’s Rule**. The hydrogen (electrophile) adds to the carbon with the most hydrogens, forming the most stable carbocation intermediate. The major product forms from this most stable carbocation.

### From Alcohols (Nucleophilic Substitution)
The hydroxyl (-OH) group is replaced by a halogen.
- **Using $HX$:** React alcohol with $HX$ (or a mixture like $KBr$ with concentrated $H_2SO_4$ or $H_3PO_4$ to make $HX$ *in situ*). **Heat** is required.
- **Using $PCl_3$:** React alcohol with $PCl_3$. Requires **Heat**. By-product is $H_3PO_3$.
- **Using $PCl_5$:** React alcohol with $PCl_5$. Occurs at **Room Temperature**. By-products are $POCl_3$ + $HCl$.
- **Using $SOCl_2$:** React alcohol with $SOCl_2$. Occurs at **Room Temperature**. By-products are $SO_2$ + $HCl$. *(Often preferred as by-products are gases and escape).*

## 3. Nucleophilic Substitution Reactions

> [!info] **Why do they react?** 
> The C-X bond is polar due to the higher electronegativity of the halogen. The carbon carries a partial positive charge ($\delta+$) and the halogen a partial negative charge ($\delta-$). A nucleophile (an electron pair donor) attacks the $\delta+$ carbon.

### Reaction with Aqueous Alkali ($NaOH$ / $KOH$)
- **Reagents:** Aqueous Sodium or Potassium Hydroxide.
- **Conditions:** **Warm / Heat under reflux**.
- **Solvent:** **Aqueous** (Water).
- **Product:** Alcohol.
- **Mechanism:** The aqueous $OH^-$ ion acts as the nucleophile, replacing the halogen.

### Reaction with Cyanide Ions ($KCN$ / $NaCN$)
- **Reagents:** Potassium or Sodium Cyanide.
- **Conditions:** **Heat under reflux**.
- **Solvent:** **Ethanolic** (dissolved in ethanol).
- **Product:** Nitrile.
- **Examiner Note (Crucial):** This reaction **adds an extra carbon atom** to the carbon chain. It is used by chemists to step up a carbon chain. Remember, the lone pair is on the **Carbon** atom of the cyanide ion ($:CN^-$).

### Reaction with Ammonia ($NH_3$)
- **Reagents:** Ammonia gas.
- **Conditions:** **Heat under pressure** in a sealed tube.
- **Solvent:** **Ethanolic** (dissolved in ethanol).
- **Product:** Primary Amine + $HBr/HCl$.
- **Examiner Note:** You MUST state that Ammonia is in **excess**. If ammonia is not in excess, the primary amine product (which is also a nucleophile) will attack another halogenoalkane molecule, forming unwanted secondary and tertiary amines.

### Reaction with Water (Hydrolysis / Aqueous $AgNO_3$)
- **Reagents:** Water (often using aqueous $AgNO_3$ as the source of water and to test for the halogen).
- **Conditions:** **Heat under reflux / Warm**.
- **Product:** Alcohol + $H^+$ + $X^-$ (which precipitates with $Ag^+$).
- **Comparing Nucleophiles (Exam Trap):** Hydrolysis with water is much **slower** than with aqueous $OH^-$. Why? The hydroxide ion ($OH^-$) is a better nucleophile because it carries a **full formal negative charge**, whereas the oxygen atom in water only carries a **partial negative charge** ($\delta-$).

## 4. Elimination Reactions
An elimination reaction involves the loss of a small molecule (hydrogen halide) from the halogenoalkane to form a double bond.

- **Reagents:** $NaOH$ or $KOH$.
- **Conditions:** **Hot (Heat under reflux)**.
- **Solvent:** **Ethanolic (Anhydrous)**.
- **Mechanism:** 
  1. The $OH^-$ acts as a **base** (not a nucleophile), removing a hydrogen atom from a carbon adjacent to the C-X bond.
  2. The C-X bond breaks heterolytically, releasing the halide ion ($X^-$).
  3. A C=C double bond forms.
- **Product:** Alkene + Water + Halide salt.
- **Examiner Trap:** If the halogen is on a non-terminal carbon (e.g., 2-bromobutane), elimination can happen on either adjacent carbon. This produces a **mixture of isomeric alkenes** (e.g., but-1-ene and but-2-ene).

## 5. Reaction Mechanisms: $S_N1$ and $S_N2$

### $S_N2$ Mechanism (Primary Halogenoalkanes)
- **Name:** Substitution, Nucleophilic, **Bimolecular** (Rate depends on the concentration of *both* the halogenoalkane and the nucleophile).
- **Steps:** A **one-step** reaction.
- **Process:** The nucleophile donates a pair of electrons to the $\delta+$ carbon while the C-X bond simultaneously breaks (heterolytic fission).
- **Transition State:** You must draw the transition state showing partial bonds (dashed lines) to both the nucleophile and the leaving group, enclosed in square brackets with a negative charge `[ ]⁻`.

### $S_N1$ Mechanism (Tertiary Halogenoalkanes)
- **Name:** Substitution, Nucleophilic, **Unimolecular** (Rate depends *only* on the concentration of the halogenoalkane).
- **Steps:** A **two-step** reaction.
- **Process:** 
  1. **Slow (rate-determining) step:** The C-X bond breaks heterolytically, forming a **tertiary carbocation intermediate** and a halide ion.
  2. **Fast step:** The nucleophile attacks the positive carbocation.

### Carbocation Stability (Explaining $S_N1$ vs $S_N2$)
- **Exam Question:** "Explain why tertiary halogenoalkanes proceed via $S_N1$."
- **Answer:** Alkyl groups have a **positive inductive effect** (they push electron density away from themselves). In a tertiary carbocation, three alkyl groups push electron density towards the positively charged carbon, reducing its charge density. This makes tertiary carbocations **much more stable** than primary carbocations. 

### Drawing the Mechanisms (Where marks are lost)
- Include all partial charges ($\delta+$ on C, $\delta-$ on Halogen).
- The curly arrow MUST start **exactly from the lone pair** of the nucleophile and point directly to the $\delta+$ carbon atom.
- The curly arrow representing bond breaking MUST start from the **middle of the C-X bond** and point directly to the Halogen atom.

## 6. Reactivity Trends

> [!error] Common Misconception
> Students often assume C-F is the most reactive because it is the most polar bond. **This is completely wrong.** Reactivity is dictated by **Bond Enthalpy (Bond Energy)**, not polarity.

- **The Trend:** Halogenoalkanes become **more reactive** as you move **down the group** (Fluoro < Chloro < Bromo < Iodo).
- **The Explanation:** As halogen atoms get larger down the group, the C-X bond gets longer. Therefore, the **bond enthalpy decreases** (the bond gets weaker).
  - **C-F bond:** Requires the most energy to break ($467 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$); strongest bond. Fluoroalkanes are very unreactive.
  - **C-I bond:** Requires the least energy to break ($228 \text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$); weakest bond. Iodoalkanes are the most reactive.
  
### Aqueous Silver Nitrate ($AgNO_3$) Test
Used to determine the relative rate of hydrolysis (nucleophilic substitution) of halogenoalkanes.
- **Fluoroalkanes:** No precipitate (bond too strong).
- **Chloroalkanes:** Forms a **White** precipitate ($AgCl$) *slowly*.
- **Bromoalkanes:** Forms a **Cream** precipitate ($AgBr$).
- **Iodoalkanes:** Forms a **Yellow** precipitate ($AgI$) *fastest*.

## 7. Past Paper Specifics & MCQ Traps to Watch Out For

- **Optical Isomerism in Mechanisms ($S_N1$):** Because the carbocation intermediate in an $S_N1$ mechanism is **planar**, the nucleophile can attack equally from above or below the plane. If the resulting product has a chiral center, this will produce a **racemic mixture** (a 50/50 mix of enantiomers) which is optically inactive.
- **Reagents vs Conditions:** Mark schemes are brutal here. If asked for "reagent and conditions", write both distinctly. Example: Reagent = $NaOH$, Conditions = *aqueous and heat*. Writing just "aqueous NaOH" only scores the reagent mark.
- **Homolytic vs Heterolytic (MCQs):** Remember that *homolytic* fission creates free radicals (no charge), while *heterolytic* fission creates ions (used in $S_N1$ and $S_N2$). 
- **Reading Skeletal Structures:** In Paper 1, you must rapidly identify 1°, 2°, and 3° halogenoalkanes from skeletal formulae. If the carbon bonded to the halogen is at a vertex with three other lines connecting to carbons, it is tertiary.
