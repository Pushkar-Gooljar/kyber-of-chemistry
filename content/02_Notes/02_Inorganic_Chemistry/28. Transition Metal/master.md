# ⚛️ Transition Elements Master Cheatsheet (CIE A-Level 9701)

> [!abstract] Overview
> This document contains high-yield, mark-scheme-approved definitions, equations, and explanations. Words in **bold** are standard marking points (AW - alternative wording usually not accepted for these).

## 1. Core Definitions & Electronic Configurations
**Definition:** A transition element is a d-block element that forms **one or more stable ions** with an **incomplete (partially filled) d-subshell**.

*   **Exceptions (The Traps):** 
    *   **Scandium (Sc):** Only forms $Sc^{3+}$. Electronic configuration is $[Ar] 3d^0$. (Empty d-subshell).
    *   **Zinc (Zn):** Only forms $Zn^{2+}$. Electronic configuration is $[Ar] 3d^{10}$. (Full d-subshell).
    *   *Neither are classified as transition elements.*
*   **Filling/Emptying Rule:** The **4s subshell fills first and empties first**. When writing ion configurations, ALWAYS remove 4s electrons before 3d electrons.
*   **Anomalies:** Chromium ($[Ar] 3d^5 4s^1$) and Copper ($[Ar] 3d^{10} 4s^1$). They move one 4s electron to the 3d subshell to achieve energetically stable half-filled or fully-filled 3d subshells.

## 2. 3d Orbital Shapes (Syllabus Requirement)
You must be able to sketch and describe specific 3d orbitals.
*   **$d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$ orbitals:** Contain four lobes that point **between** the axes. 
*   **$d_{x^2-y^2}$ orbital:** Contains four lobes that point directly **along** the x and y axes.
*   **$d_{z^2}$ orbital:** Has a **dumbbell shape** pointing directly **along** the z-axis, with a "doughnut" ring around the center.

## 3. Key Chemical Properties
Transition metals exhibit four characteristic properties due to the similar energies of the 3d and 4s subshells:
1.  **Variable Oxidation States:** Due to the small energy difference between 3d and 4s electrons, varying numbers of electrons can be lost.
2.  **Formation of Complex Ions:** Possess **energetically accessible vacant d-orbitals** that can accept lone pairs from ligands.
3.  **Colored Compounds:** Due to d-d electron transitions between non-degenerate d-orbitals.
4.  **Catalytic Activity:** 
    *   **Homogeneous Catalysis:** Transition elements have variable oxidation states, allowing them to transfer electrons, act as reaction intermediates, and easily switch between states (e.g., $Fe^{2+}/Fe^{3+}$ in the $S_2O_8^{2-} / I^-$ reaction).
    *   **Heterogeneous Catalysis:** Vacant d-orbitals allow reactants to **adsorb** onto the solid metal surface. This **weakens bonds** in the reactant molecules, lowering the activation energy, holding them in the correct orientation, and then they **desorb** (e.g., solid Fe in the Haber process).

## 4. Ligands & Complex Ions

**Definitions:**
*   **Complex Ion:** A central metal atom/ion surrounded by ligands bonded via dative covalent (coordinate) bonds.
*   **Ligand:** A molecule or ion with at least one **lone pair of electrons** capable of forming a **dative covalent bond** to a metal ion.
*   **Coordination Number:** The **total number of dative covalent bonds** formed between the central metal ion and the ligands (NOT necessarily the number of ligands).

### Types of Ligands
| Type | Definition | Common Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Monodentate** | Donates 1 lone pair | $H_2O$, $NH_3$, $Cl^-$, $CN^-$, $OH^-$, $SCN^-$ |
| **Bidentate** | Donates 2 lone pairs | **en** (1,2-diaminoethane: $H_2NCH_2CH_2NH_2$), **ox** (ethanedioate: $C_2O_4^{2-}$) |
| **Polydentate** | Donates >2 lone pairs | $EDTA^{4-}$ (Hexadentate - donates 6 lone pairs) |

### Geometry & Shapes (Mark Scheme Standard)
| Coord. No. | Shape | Bond Angle | Typical Complexes |
| :---: | :--- | :---: | :--- |
| **6** | Octahedral | $90^\circ$ | $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$, $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$, $[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}$ |
| **4** | Tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ | Large ligands (high repulsion): $[CuCl_4]^{2-}$, $[CoCl_4]^{2-}$ |
| **4** | Square Planar | $90^\circ$ | Platinum complexes: $Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2$ (Cisplatin) |
| **2** | Linear | $180^\circ$ | Silver complexes: $[Ag(NH_3)_2]^+$ (Tollens'), $[Ag(CN)_2]^-$ |

## 5. The "Holy Grail" Correction: Deprotonation vs. Ligand Exchange

> [!warning] EXAMINER TRAP: Deprotonation vs Ligand Exchange
> Many textbooks incorrectly label the addition of aqueous $NaOH$ or dilute aqueous $NH_3$ to a hexaaqua ion as "ligand exchange". **It is NOT.** It is an **acid-base reaction (deprotonation / precipitation)** where the $OH^-$ or $NH_3$ acts as a base, removing protons from the water ligands to form a neutral precipitate. 
> 
> **Ligand exchange** ONLY occurs when bonds between the metal and the ligand are broken and replaced (e.g., adding **excess concentrated** $NH_3$ or $Cl^-$).

### A. Acidity of Hexaaqua Ions (Hydrolysis)
*   Aqueous $M^{3+}$ ions (like $Fe^{3+}$) are highly charged and polarize the $O-H$ bonds in the water ligands. 
*   They act as Brønsted-Lowry acids, releasing $H^+$ into solution (creating acidic solutions):
    *   $[Fe(H_2O)_6]^{3+}_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons [Fe(H_2O)_5(OH)]^{2+}_{(aq)} + H^+_{(aq)}$

### B. Copper(II) Chemistry
*   **Aqueous Ion:** $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ (Pale blue solution)
*   **Add dilute $NaOH$ or limited $NH_3$ (DEPROTONATION):**
    *   Equation: $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons Cu(H_2O)_4(OH)_{2 (s)} + 2H_2O_{(l)}$
    *   Observation: **Pale blue precipitate**.
*   **Add EXCESS conc. $NH_3$ (LIGAND EXCHANGE):**
    *   Equation: $Cu(H_2O)_4(OH)_{2 (s)} + 4NH_{3 (aq)} \rightleftharpoons [Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}_{(aq)} + 2H_2O_{(l)} + 2OH^-_{(aq)}$
    *   Observation: Precipitate dissolves to form a **deep blue solution**. (Coordination number remains 6, shape is octahedral).
*   **Add conc. $HCl$ (LIGAND EXCHANGE):**
    *   Equation: $[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}_{(aq)} + 4Cl^-_{(aq)} \rightleftharpoons [CuCl_4]^{2-}_{(aq)} + 6H_2O_{(l)}$
    *   Observation: Pale blue solution turns **yellow** (appears green in transition due to blue + yellow mixing). 
    *   *Note:* Coordination number changes from 6 to 4 because $Cl^-$ is a larger ligand and they repel each other more strongly. Geometry changes from Octahedral to Tetrahedral.

### C. Cobalt(II) Chemistry
*   **Aqueous Ion:** $[Co(H_2O)_6]^{2+}$ (Pink solution)
*   **Add dilute $NaOH$ or limited $NH_3$ (DEPROTONATION):**
    *   Observation: **Blue precipitate** (which turns red/pink on warming or standing). Formula: $Co(H_2O)_4(OH)_{2 (s)}$.
*   **Add EXCESS conc. $NH_3$ (LIGAND EXCHANGE):**
    *   Observation: Precipitate dissolves to form a **brown solution** ($[Co(NH_3)_6]^{2+}$). 
    *   *Redox note:* The $Co(II)$ complex is unstable and oxidizes in air to form the $Co(III)$ complex $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$ (yellow/orange).
*   **Add conc. $HCl$ (LIGAND EXCHANGE):**
    *   Observation: Pink solution turns into a **blue solution** ($[CoCl_4]^{2-}$). Tetrahedral shape.

## 6. Origin of Colour in Complexes (5-Mark Standard Answer)

> [!important] Orbital Splitting Patterns (Frequently Tested)
> **Octahedral complexes:** The 6 ligands approach *along* the x, y, and z axes. Therefore, electrons in the $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$ orbitals experience **greater repulsion**, splitting the 3d subshell so **2 orbitals are at a higher energy** and **3 orbitals are at a lower energy**.
> **Tetrahedral complexes:** The 4 ligands approach *between* the axes. The $d_{xy}, d_{xz}, d_{yz}$ orbitals experience **greater repulsion**, splitting the 3d subshell so **3 orbitals are at a higher energy** and **2 orbitals are at a lower energy**.

If asked "Explain why transition element complexes are colored" write exactly this sequence:
1.  In an isolated atom, the five 3d orbitals are **degenerate** (equal in energy).
2.  In a complex, ligands donate lone pairs. The repulsion from these lone pairs causes the 3d orbitals to **split into two sets of non-degenerate orbitals** with an energy gap, **$\Delta E$**.
3.  Electrons in the lower energy d-orbitals **absorb energy** from the **visible light spectrum**.
4.  This exact energy promotes an electron to a higher energy d-orbital (**d-d transition**). *Equation to cite: $\Delta E = h\nu$* (where $h$ is Planck's constant, $\nu$ is frequency).
5.  The color observed is the **complementary color** (the frequencies of visible light that are *not* absorbed / are transmitted).

*Factors changing the color (changing $\Delta E$):* 
1. Oxidation state of the metal. 2. Identity of the metal. 3. Identity of the ligand. 4. Coordination number / Geometry.

## 7. Stability Constants ($K_{stab}$)

**Definition:** The equilibrium constant for the formation of a complex ion in a solvent from its constituent ions or molecules.
*   **Expression Rule:** Written exactly like $K_c$. $[H_2O]$ is **omitted** from the expression because it is the solvent and its concentration is effectively constant/in excess.
*   **Interpretation:** A higher $K_{stab}$ value (or more positive $\log_{10}K_{stab}$) means the equilibrium lies further to the right $\rightarrow$ the complex is **more stable**.

> [!info] The Chelate Effect (Entropy)
> Replacing monodentate ligands with bidentate/polydentate ligands causes a massive increase in stability. 
> *Example:* $[Ni(H_2O)_6]^{2+} + 3\text{en} \rightleftharpoons [Ni(\text{en})_3]^{2+} + 6H_2O$
> *   **Explanation:** 4 reacting particles generate 7 product particles. There is a massive increase in the number of particles in solution, leading to a large **increase in entropy ($\Delta S > 0$)**. Therefore, the reaction is highly feasible, making polydentate complexes incredibly stable.

## 8. Stereoisomerism in Complexes

### A. Geometrical (cis-trans) Isomerism
*   **Square Planar:** $Pt(NH_3)_2Cl_2$. 
    *   **Cisplatin:** Identical ligands are adjacent ($90^\circ$). Used as an anti-cancer drug (binds to DNA, preventing replication). **Polar**.
    *   **Transplatin:** Identical ligands are opposite ($180^\circ$). Inactive in cancer treatment. **Non-polar** (dipoles cancel).
*   **Octahedral:** Requires complexes of type $MA_4B_2$ (e.g., $[Co(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}$) or $M(\text{bidentate})_2B_2$. 
    *   *Cis:* The two identical ligands are $90^\circ$ apart.
    *   *Trans:* The two identical ligands are $180^\circ$ apart (**Non-polar** due to symmetry).

### B. Optical Isomerism
*   Occurs primarily in **octahedral complexes containing bidentate ligands**.
*   **Criteria:** Must have **non-superimposable mirror images** (enantiomers) and lack a plane of symmetry.
*   **Classic Example:** $[Ni(\text{en})_3]^{2+}$ or $[Fe(C_2O_4)_3]^{3-}$.
*   *Property:* Rotate the plane of plane-polarized light in opposite directions.

## 9. Redox Reactions & Feasibility

We use standard electrode potentials ($E^\theta$) to predict feasibility. 
*   **Rule:** The species with the **more positive $E^\theta$** will be reduced (gains electrons / acts as the oxidizing agent). 
*   Reaction is feasible if $E^\theta_{\text{cell}} = E^\theta_{\text{reduction}} - E^\theta_{\text{oxidation}} > 0$. *(Note: Very large activation energies can stop feasible reactions from occurring in reality).*

### System 1: Permanganate ($MnO_4^-$) and Iron(II) ($Fe^{2+}$)
*   **Acidic Conditions required** (usually $H_2SO_4$).
*   **Half equations:**
    *   $MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5e^- \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O$
    *   $Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Fe^{3+} + e^-$
*   **Overall:** $MnO_4^- + 8H^+ + 5Fe^{2+} \rightarrow Mn^{2+} + 4H_2O + 5Fe^{3+}$
*   **Observation:** Titrate until a **permanent pale pink** color appears (self-indicating).

### System 2: Permanganate ($MnO_4^-$) and Oxalate ($C_2O_4^{2-}$) - AUTOCATALYSIS
*   **Overall:** $2MnO_4^- + 16H^+ + 5C_2O_4^{2-} \rightarrow 2Mn^{2+} + 8H_2O + 10CO_2$
*   **Examiner Gold:** This reaction exhibits **autocatalysis**. The reaction is initially slow (high activation energy due to repulsion between two negative ions, $MnO_4^-$ and $C_2O_4^{2-}$). As $Mn^{2+}$ is produced, it acts as a catalyst, and the reaction rate increases exponentially.
*   *Mechanism:* $Mn^{2+}$ changes oxidation state between $+2$ and $+3$ to transfer electrons between the negative ions.

### System 3: Copper(II) and Iodide (Iodometric Titration)
*   Used to find the concentration of $Cu^{2+}$ in solution.
*   **Step 1:** Add excess $KI$ to $Cu^{2+}$. 
    *   $2Cu^{2+} + 4I^- \rightarrow 2CuI_{(s)} + I_{2 (aq)}$
    *   *Observation:* A **white precipitate** ($CuI$) forms in a **brown solution** (iodine). 
*   **Step 2:** Titrate the liberated iodine against standard Sodium Thiosulfate ($S_2O_3^{2-}$).
    *   $I_2 + 2S_2O_3^{2-} \rightarrow 2I^- + S_4O_6^{2-}$
    *   *Indicator:* Add **starch** near the end-point (when solution fades to pale yellow). Solution turns blue-black, titrate until colorless.
*   *Ratio:* 1 mole of $Cu^{2+}$ produces 0.5 moles of $I_2$, which reacts with 1 mole of $S_2O_3^{2-}$. Therefore, **$n(Cu^{2+}) = n(S_2O_3^{2-})$**.

### System 4: Iron(III) and Iodide (Common Exam Reaction)
*   **Overall Equation:** $2Fe^{3+} + 2I^- \rightleftharpoons 2Fe^{2+} + I_2$
*   *Observation:* The yellow/orange solution of $Fe^{3+}$ turns brown due to the formation of aqueous $I_2$. Can also be titrated against Thiosulfate.
