# 🏆 Transition Elements Ultimate Master Checklist

## 1. General Physical and Chemical Properties
### Definitions & Core Concepts
- [x] **Define a transition element:** A d-block element which forms one or more stable ions with an **incomplete (partially filled) d-subshell/d-orbital**.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Do not confuse the definition of the element with the reason it forms complexes. The definition *must* mention the stable ion with an incomplete d-subshell.
- [x] **State typical chemical properties of transition elements:** Variable oxidation states, act as catalysts, form complex ions, form coloured compounds.
- [x] **Explain variable oxidation states:** The 3d and 4s sub-shells/orbitals are **close/similar in energy**.
  - ***Inference: You may be asked to explain why s-block elements (like Ca) do NOT show variable oxidation states (because the energy gap between their outer s-orbital and the next inner orbital is too large).***
- [x] **Write electronic configurations:** Use [Ar] shorthand. Remember 4s electrons are lost *first* when forming ions.
  - *Pitfall:* Fe is `[Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²`. Fe³⁺ is `[Ar] 3d⁵`. Do not write `4s² 3d³` for Fe³⁺! Cu is `[Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹` and Cu²⁺ is `[Ar] 3d⁹`.

### Orbital Shapes
- [x] **Define degenerate orbitals:** Orbitals that have the **same energy**.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Never define this as "non-degenerate". Do not say "similar energy"—they must be exactly the *same* energy.
- [x] **Define non-degenerate orbitals:** Orbitals that do *not* have the same energy / have different energies.
- [x] **Sketch the $3d_{xy}$ orbital:** Draw four lobes situated exactly **between** the x and y axes.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* If you draw the lobes *on* the axes, you will lose the mark (that is the $d_{x^2-y^2}$ orbital).
- [x] **Sketch the $3d_{z^2}$ orbital:** Draw two lobes directly on the z-axis with a ring/doughnut shape centered in the xy-plane around the nucleus.
  - ***Inference: Even though $3d_{xy}$ and $3d_{z^2}$ are explicitly mentioned in the syllabus, you should be able to recognise or sketch all 5 d-orbitals.***

### Catalysis
- [x] **Explain why transition elements behave as catalysts (General):** 
  1. They have **more than one stable oxidation state** (variable oxidation states).
  2. They have **empty/vacant d-orbitals** that are **energetically accessible** to accept lone pairs/form dative bonds with ligands.
- [ ] **Describe the mode of action of a heterogeneous catalyst (e.g., Pt in catalytic converters, Fe in Haber process):**
  1. **Adsorption** of reactants onto the catalyst surface.
  2. Bonds **within** the reactant molecules are weakened.
  3. **Desorption** of the products.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Do NOT say "intermolecular forces are weakened" or "bonds *between* reactants are weakened". You must specify that the covalent bonds *within* the reactant molecules are weakened.
- [ ] **Provide equations for homogeneous catalysis:** e.g., NO₂ catalysing the oxidation of SO₂.
  - Step 1: NO₂ + SO₂ $\rightarrow$ NO + SO₃
  - Step 2: 2NO + O₂ $\rightarrow$ 2NO₂
- [ ] **Provide equations for homogeneous catalysis (Fe³⁺ or Fe²⁺ with I⁻ and S₂O₈²⁻):**
  - Step 1: 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ $\rightarrow$ 2Fe²⁺ + I₂
  - Step 2: 2Fe²⁺ + S₂O₈²⁻ $\rightarrow$ 2Fe³⁺ + 2SO₄²⁻
  - ***Inference: Be prepared to use $E^\circ$ values to prove WHY a specific transition metal ion can act as a catalyst (the $E^\circ$ of the catalyst half-cell must lie BETWEEN the $E^\circ$ values of the two reactant half-cells).***

## 2. Reactions and Complex Ion Formation
### Ligands & Complexes
- [x] **Define a complex ion:** A molecule or ion formed by a **central metal atom/ion** surrounded by/bonded to one or more ligands.
- [x] **Define a ligand:** A species that contains a **lone pair of electrons** that forms a **dative covalent (coordinate) bond** to a central metal atom/ion.
- [x] **Define coordination number:** The total number of **dative/co-ordinate bonds** being formed by the metal ion.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Do not say "number of ligands". A bidentate ligand forms *two* coordinate bonds, so 3 bidentate ligands = a coordination number of 6.
- [ ] **Categorise ligands by denticity:**
  - **Monodentate** (donates 1 lone pair): H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, CN⁻.
  - **Bidentate** (donates 2 lone pairs): 1,2-diaminoethane (en), ethanedioate (C₂O₄²⁻).
  - **Polydentate/Hexadentate** (donates >2 lone pairs): EDTA⁴⁻. (Donates 6 lone pairs: 2 from N, 4 from O⁻).
- [x] **Explain why transition elements form complex ions:** They have **vacant d-orbitals** that are **energetically accessible** to accept lone pairs from ligands.

### Specific Aqueous Reactions (Memorise these!)
- [ ] **Copper (Cu²⁺) Reactions:**
  - Starting material: `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺` (Pale blue solution).
  - Add dropwise NaOH or NH₃: Forms `Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂` or `Cu(OH)₂` (**Pale blue precipitate**). *Acid-base / Deprotonation / Precipitation reaction.*
  - Add excess NH₃: Forms `[Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺` (**Deep/dark blue solution**). *Ligand exchange reaction.*
  - Add excess conc. HCl: Forms `[CuCl₄]²⁻` (**Yellow solution**). *Ligand exchange reaction. Note the change in geometry from octahedral to tetrahedral.*
- [ ] **Cobalt (Co²⁺) Reactions:**
  - Starting material: `[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺` (Pink solution).
  - Add dropwise NaOH: Forms `Co(H₂O)₄(OH)₂` or `Co(OH)₂` (**Blue/Pink precipitate**).
  - Add excess NH₃: Forms `[Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺` (**Straw/yellow-brown solution**).
  - Add excess conc. HCl: Forms `[CoCl₄]²⁻` (**Blue solution**).
  - *Examiner Nuance:* You MUST balance your equations perfectly, including charges and the correct number of displaced waters/hydroxides. e.g., `[Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 2OH⁻ -> Co(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ + 2H₂O`.
- [ ] **Tollens' Reagent Synthesis (Silver Complexes):**
  - Step 1: `2Ag⁺ + 2OH⁻ -> Ag₂O(s) + H₂O` (Brown precipitate forms).
  - Step 2: `Ag₂O(s) + 4NH₃ + H₂O -> 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 2OH⁻` (Colourless solution forms).
  - ***Inference: They frequently ask for the bond angles of [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺. The N-Ag-N bond is 180° (linear), but the H-N-Ag bond angle is 109.5° (tetrahedral around the Nitrogen atom).***

### Redox Titrations & $E^\circ$ Calculations
- [ ] **Manganate(VII) / Ethanedioate (MnO₄⁻ / C₂O₄²⁻):**
  - Ratio: 2 MnO₄⁻ reacts with 5 C₂O₄²⁻.
  - Conditions: Acidic, heating required.
  - Eq: `2MnO₄⁻ + 5C₂O₄²⁻ + 16H⁺ -> 2Mn²⁺ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O`
- [ ] **Manganate(VII) / Iron(II) (MnO₄⁻ / Fe²⁺):**
  - Ratio: 1 MnO₄⁻ reacts with 5 Fe²⁺.
  - Eq: `MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ -> Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O`
- [ ] **Copper(II) / Iodide (Cu²⁺ / I⁻):**
  - Reaction 1: `2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ -> 2CuI(s) + I₂(aq)` (White ppt of CuI in brown I₂ solution).
  - Reaction 2 (Titration): `I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ -> 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻`
  - Overall mole ratio: 1 mol S₂O₃²⁻ = 1 mol Cu²⁺.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Pay incredibly close attention to dilution factors in these questions (e.g., if you titrate 25cm³ out of a 250cm³ volumetric flask, you MUST multiply your final moles by 10).

## 3. Colour of Complexes
- [ ] **Explain the origin of colour in transition metal complexes:**
  1. The d-orbitals **split into two different energy levels** (non-degenerate sets) due to repulsion from ligand electrons.
  2. Electrons absorb energy (a photon) and are **promoted/excited** from a lower d-orbital to a higher d-orbital (d-d transition).
  3. A specific **frequency/wavelength of visible light is absorbed**.
  4. The colour observed is the **complementary colour** (the light that is transmitted/reflected).
- [ ] **Explain why different complexes of the same metal have different colours:**
  - Different ligands cause a **different energy gap ($\Delta E$)** between the split d-orbitals.
  - Therefore, a **different frequency/wavelength** of visible light is absorbed.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Do not just say "$\Delta E$ changes". You must state "they have a *different* $\Delta E$".
- [ ] **Explain why Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺, Cu⁺, Ag⁺, and Zn²⁺ complexes are colourless:**
  - Ions like Cu⁺, Ag⁺, Zn²⁺ have a **full d-subshell (d¹⁰)**.
  - Ions like Sc³⁺, Ti⁴⁺ have an **empty d-subshell (d⁰)**.
  - Therefore, **d-d transitions are not possible** / no d-electrons can be promoted.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* You must explicitly state *both* that the subshell is full/empty AND that d-d transitions cannot occur.

### Splitting Patterns
- [ ] **Octahedral splitting:** 2 orbitals at higher energy, 3 orbitals at lower energy.
- [ ] **Tetrahedral splitting:** 3 orbitals at higher energy, 2 orbitals at lower energy.

## 4. Stereoisomerism in Complexes
- [ ] **Recognise Geometrical (cis/trans) isomerism:**
  - Square planar: e.g., `[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]` (cis-platin and trans-platin).
  - Octahedral (with monodentate ligands): e.g., `[Co(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺`.
  - Octahedral (with bidentate ligands): e.g., `[Ni(en)₂Cl₂]`.
- [ ] **Recognise Optical isomerism:**
  - Octahedral with 3 bidentate ligands: e.g., `[Cr(en)₃]³⁺` (forms non-superimposable mirror images).
  - Octahedral with 2 bidentate ligands in the **cis** configuration: e.g., `cis-[Ru(en)₂Cl₂]⁺`.
  - ***Inference: Be able to spot that the TRANS isomer of a complex like `[Ru(en)₂Cl₂]⁺` does NOT show optical isomerism because it has a plane of symmetry.***
- [ ] **Determine Polarity:**
  - **Trans** isomers are generally **non-polar** because the dipoles / polar bonds / partial charges cancel out.
  - **Cis** isomers are generally **polar** because the dipoles do not cancel out (the molecule is asymmetric).
  - *Examiner Nuance:* When explaining why a trans isomer is non-polar, specify that the "dipoles cancel" or "partial charges cancel". Do not just say "bonds cancel".
- [ ] **Drawing 3D Isomers:**
  - Use wedges (coming out) and dashes (going in) correctly.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Ensure your octahedral drawings actually look octahedral. The axial bonds must be 180° to each other (vertical line), and the equatorial bonds must be shown with wedges and dashes crossing the center. Do not draw distorted shapes.

## 5. Stability Constants ($K_{stab}$)
- [ ] **Define stability constant ($K_{stab}$):** The equilibrium constant for the formation of a complex in a solvent from its constituent ions or molecules.
- [ ] **Write expressions for $K_{stab}$:** 
  - E.g., for `[Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4NH₃ ⇌ [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ + 4H₂O`
  - $K_{stab} = \frac{[[Cu(NH_3)_4(H_2O)_2]^{2+}]}{[[Cu(H_2O)_6]^{2+}][NH_3]^4}$
  - *Crucial Rule:* **Water ([H₂O]) is NEVER included** in the $K_{stab}$ expression because it is the solvent and its concentration is effectively constant.
  - *Examiner Nuance:* Put the charge of the complex *inside* the outer concentration brackets. e.g., `[[Fe(SCN)]²⁺]`, not `[Fe(SCN)]²⁺`.
- [ ] **Interpret $K_{stab}$ values:**
  - A higher/larger $K_{stab}$ value means the complex is **more stable**.
  - Ligand exchange reactions will always favour the formation of the complex with the **larger $K_{stab}$ value**.
- [ ] **Perform $K_{stab}$ calculations:**
  - Use given equilibrium concentrations to find $K_{stab}$.
  - Given $K_{stab}$ and initial concentrations, calculate the concentration of a specific ion at equilibrium.
  - ***Inference: Always check the units of $K_{stab}$ in calculations. You may be asked to deduce the units of $K_{stab}$ for a specific complex formation (e.g., $mol^{-4} dm^{12}$).***
