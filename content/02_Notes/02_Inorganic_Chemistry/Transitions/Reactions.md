# Copper

>[!info] Copper(II) + Dropwise $\ce{NaOH}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pale blue solution}} + \ce{2OH^- ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(H2O)4(OH)2]}}_{\text{(s) Pale blue precipitate}} + \ce{2H2O}$$
>## Mechanism 
>![Deprotonation Reaction|500](copper_ii_hexaaqua_getting_graped.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- Negatively charged Bronsted-Lowry base directly abstracts hydrogen atoms from the $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands.
>>- Due to the Jahn-Teller effect the axial bonds are longer, it is more energetically favourable to have the resulting negatively charged $\ce{OH-}$ ligands on the shorter equatorial bonds so they are closer to the positively charged central metal ion.
>>- After two $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands get deprotonated, the central metal ion loses its charge. It no longer pulls electron density towards it making the remaining bonds stronger.
>>- That is why reaction stops after two deprotonations.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Deprotonation
>- Acid-Base
>- Precipitation
>## Observations
>- **Pale blue solution** forms a **pale blue precipitate**
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Why is a precipitate formed
>>The complex loses its charge (becomes neutral), so it can no longer form strong ion-dipole bonds with water molecules.

>[!info] $\ce{[Cu(H_{2}O)6]^2+}$+ Excess $\ce{NH_{3}}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pale blue solution}} + \ce{4NH3 ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Deep blue solution}} + \ce{4H2O}$$
>## Mechanism
>![copper_2_hexaaqua_nh3_ligand_exhange|500](copper_2_hexaaqua_nh3_ligand_exhange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- $\ce{NH_{3}}$ is a stronger ligand than $\ce{H_{2}O}$
>>- So, $\ce{Cu - NH_{3}}$ bonds are stronger than $\ce{Cu - OH_{2}}$.
>>- It is energetically favourable for $\ce{NH_{3}}$ to substitute $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands as forming the strong $\ce{Cu - NH_{3}}$ bonds releases a lot of energy.
>>- Due to the Jahn-Teller effect, the axial bolds are longer and weaker, it is energetically unfavourable to substitute those $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands.
>>- Therefore the most energetically stable configuration is 4 $\ce{NH_{3}}$ ligands on the equatorial positions.
>>- **NO MATTER WHAT THAT IS WHAT HAPPENS WITH A $\ce{Cu^2+}$ COMPLEX IN AQUEOUS MEDIUM**
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Pale blue solution** turns into a **deep blue solution**


>[!info] $\ce{[Cu(H_{2}O)4(OH)2]^2+}$+ Excess $\ce{NH_{3}}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(H2O)4(OH)2]}}_{\text{(s) Pale blue precipitate}} + \ce{4NH3 ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Deep blue solution}} + \ce{2H2O + 2OH-}$$
>## Mechanism
>![tetraaquadihydroxocupper_2_nh3_ligand_exchange|500](tetraaquadihydroxocupper_2_nh3_ligand_exchange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- Copper (II) complexes are labile, this means that the bonds break and reform many times per second.
>>- It is more energetically favourable for the $\ce{OH-}$ ligands to be in the equatorial positions opposite to each other to minimise repulsion between them and maximise attraction to $\ce{Cu^2+}$.
>>- $\ce{NH_{3}}$ is a stronger ligand than $\ce{H_{2}O}$ and $\ce{OH-}$
>>- So, $\ce{Cu - NH_{3}}$ bonds are stronger than $\ce{Cu - OH_{2}}$ and $\ce{Cu - OH-}$.
>>- It is energetically favourable for $\ce{NH_{3}}$ to substitute $\ce{H_{2}O}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ ligands as forming the strong $\ce{Cu - NH_{3}}$ bonds releases a lot of energy.
>>- Due to the Jahn-Teller effect, the axial bolds are longer and weaker, it is energetically unfavourable to substitute those $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands.
>>- Therefore the most energetically stable configuration is 4 $\ce{NH_{3}}$ ligands on the equatorial positions.
>>- **NO MATTER WHAT THAT IS WHAT HAPPENS WITH A $\ce{Cu^2+}$ COMPLEX IN AQUEOUS MEDIUM**
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Pale blue precipitate** dissolves to form a **deep blue solution**
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip]
>>- The precipitate dissolves because the resulting complex is charged and can form ion-dipole bonds with water molecules.

>[!info] Copper(II) + Concentrated $\ce{HCl}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Cu(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pale blue solution}} + \ce{4Cl- <=>}\ \underbrace{\ce{[CuCl4]^2-}}_{\text{(aq) Yellow-green solution}} + \ce{6H2O}$$
>## Mechanism
>![copper_2_hexaaqua_cl_ligand_exchange|500](copper_2_hexaaqua_cl_ligand_exchange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- $\ce{Cl-}$ is a much larger ligand than $\ce{H_{2}O}$.
>>- There is only enough space to have 4 $\ce{Cl-}$ ligands
>>- Additionally, since the $\ce{Cl-}$ ligands are negative charged, they repel each other and adopt a tetrahedral shape to minimise repulsion.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Pale blue solution** turns into a **yellow solution**
>- (*Often appears green during transition because blue + yellow = green, but the final mark scheme answer is yellow*).
>- The colour can be changed through Le Chatelier's principle since the reaction is reversible:
>	- Adding water favours the backwards reaction in order to decrease water concentration. Making the solution more blue.
>	- Adding more $\ce{Cl-}$ favours the forwards reaction in order to decrease $\ce{Cl-}$ concentration. This makes the solution more yellow.
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Tip
>>- Coordination number changes from **6** to **4**.
>>- The geometry changes from **octahedral** to **tetrahedral**. This is because the $\ce{Cl-}$ ligands are larger and only 4 of them can fit. Additionally, they adopt a tetrahedral shape to minimise repulsion between them.
>>- $\ce{Cl-}$ is a weaker ligand than $\ce{H_{2}O}$, to overcome this **concentrated** $\ce{HCl}$ must be used.
>>- This reaction is energetically feasible because 5 particles (1 complex + 4$\ce{Cl-}$) turn into 7 particles (1 complex + 6 $\ce{H_{2}O}$). There is an increase in entropy.

# Cobalt

>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Dropwise $\ce{NaOH}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pink solution}} + \ce{2OH^- ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)4(OH)2]}}_{\text{(s) Blue precipitate}} + \ce{2H2O}$$
>## Mechanism 
>![cobalt_ii_hexaaqua_deprotonation|500](cobalt_ii_hexaaqua_getting_graped.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- The negatively charged Brønsted–Lowry base ($\ce{OH-}$) directly abstracts hydrogen ions ($\ce{H+}$) from the coordinated $\ce{H2O}$ ligands.
>>- Unlike $\ce{Cu^2+}$, $\ce{Co^2+}$ has a negligible Jahn–Teller effect ($e_g$ orbitals are symmetrically half-filled, $t_{2g}^5 e_g^2$), so all six coordination sites are equivalent in length.
>>- After two $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands get deprotonated, the central metal ion loses its charge. It no longer pulls electron density towards it making the remaining bonds stronger.
>>- That is why reaction stops after two deprotonations.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Deprotonation
>- Acid-Base
>- Precipitation
>## Observations
>- **Pink solution** forms a **blue precipitate** (which may turn pink/red on standing, but *blue precipitate* is the primary mark scheme answer).
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Why is a precipitate formed?
>>The complex loses its charge (becomes neutral), so it can no longer form strong ion-dipole interactions with water molecules and precipitates out of solution.
>>- *Examiner Nuance:* This is an **acid-base / deprotonation** reaction, **not** a ligand exchange reaction.


>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Dropwise $\ce{NH_{3}}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pink solution}} + \ce{2NH3 ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)4(OH)2]}}_{\text{(s) Blue precipitate}} + \ce{2NH4^+}$$
>## Mechanism
>![cobalt_ii_hexaaqua_nh3_deprotonation|500](cobalt_ii_hexaaqua_getting_graped.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- In dilute / dropwise conditions, aqueous ammonia acts as a Brønsted–Lowry base, abstracting protons ($\ce{H+}$) from the coordinated water molecules.
>>- After two $\ce{H_{2}O}$ ligands get deprotonated, the central metal ion loses its charge. It no longer pulls electron density towards it making the remaining bonds stronger.
>>- That is why reaction stops after two deprotonations.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Deprotonation
>- Acid-Base
>- Precipitation
>## Observations
>- **Pink solution** forms a **blue precipitate**.
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Why is a precipitate formed?
>>The complex becomes neutral ($\ce{[Co(H2O)4(OH)2]}^0$), losing its ability to form favourable ion-dipole bonds with bulk solvent.


>[!info] $\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}$ + Excess $\ce{NH3}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pink solution}} + \ce{6NH3 ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Straw / yellow-brown solution}} + \ce{6H2O}$$
>## Mechanism
>![cobalt_2_hexaaqua_nh3_ligand_exchange|500](cobalt_2_hexaaqua_nh3_ligand_exchange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- $\ce{NH3}$ is a stronger ligand (better $\sigma$-donor) than $\ce{H2O}$, so $\ce{Co - NH3}$ bonds are stronger than $\ce{Co - OH2}$ bonds.
>>- Because $\ce{Co^2+}$ does not exhibit significant Jahn–Teller distortion, all six bond lengths are roughly equal.
>>- There are no weakened axial bonds to inhibit full substitution; hence, $\ce{NH3}$ replaces **all six** water ligands.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Pink solution** turns into a **straw / yellow-brown solution**.
>## Notes & Nuances 
>>[!tip]
>>***If this yellow-brown solution is left to stand in air, it darkens to a brown/black solution. Why? Because the Co²⁺ complex is easily oxidised by oxygen in the air to form a stable Co³⁺ complex: `[Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺`.***


>[!info] $\ce{[Co(H2O)4(OH)2]}$ + Excess $\ce{NH3}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)4(OH)2]}}_{\text{(s) Blue precipitate}} + \ce{6NH3 ->} \underbrace{\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Straw / yellow-brown solution}} + \ce{4H2O + 2OH-}$$
>## Mechanism
>![tetraaquadihydroxocobalt_2_nh3_ligand_exchange|500](tetraaquadihydroxocobalt_2_nh3_ligand_exchange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- Cobalt(II) complexes are labile, meaning metal–ligand coordinate bonds break and reform rapidly.
>>- $\ce{NH3}$ is a neutral, stronger ligand than $\ce{H2O}$ and displaces both the $\ce{OH-}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ ligands.
>>- Unlike Copper(II), which stops at 4 $\ce{NH3}$ due to severe tetragonal elongation, Cobalt(II) substitutes at all 6 octahedral sites.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Blue precipitate** dissolves to form a **straw / yellow-brown solution**.
>- On standing in air, the solution darkens to a **brown / dark brown solution**.
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Dissolution & Oxidation Nuances
>>- **Why it dissolves:** The resulting complex ion carries a $2+$ charge, allowing it to form strong ion-dipole attractions with water.
>>- **Examiner Nuance (Air Oxidation):** On exposure to air, $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^2+}$ is readily oxidised by atmospheric oxygen to $\ce{[Co(NH3)6]^3+}$:
>>  $$\ce{4[Co(NH3)6]^2+ + O2 + 2H2O -> 4[Co(NH3)6]^3+ + 4OH-}$$
>>  This causes the solution to darken from yellow-brown to deep brown.


>[!info] Cobalt(II) + Concentrated $\ce{HCl}$
>## Equation
>$$\large \underbrace{\ce{[Co(H2O)6]^2+}}_{\text{(aq) Pink solution}} + \ce{4Cl- <=>}\ \underbrace{\ce{[CoCl4]^2-}}_{\text{(aq) Blue solution}} + \ce{6H2O}$$
>## Mechanism
>![cobalt_2_hexaaqua_cl_ligand_exchange|500](cobalt_2_hexaaqua_cl_ligand_exchange.svg)
>>[!tip]
>>- $\ce{Cl-}$ is a larger ligand than $\ce{H2O}$.
>>- Due to steric hindrance and electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged chloride ions, only 4 $\ce{Cl-}$ ligands can fit around the $\ce{Co^2+}$ ion.
>>- The complex adopts a **tetrahedral** geometry to minimise repulsion.
>## Type of Reaction
>- Ligand Exchange
>## Observations
>- **Pink solution** turns into a **blue solution**.
>- The reaction is in dynamic equilibrium and demonstrates Le Chatelier's principle:
>	- **Adding water:** shifts equilibrium backwards (left) $\rightarrow$ solution turns **pink**.
>	- **Adding conc. $\ce{HCl}$ / $\ce{Cl-}$:** shifts equilibrium forwards (right) $\rightarrow$ solution turns **blue**.
>	- **Heating the mixture:** shifts equilibrium forwards (right) because the forward reaction is **endothermic** ($\Delta H > 0$) $\rightarrow$ turns **deeper blue**.
>	- **Cooling the mixture:** shifts equilibrium backwards (left) $\rightarrow$ turns **pink**.
>## Notes & Nuances
>>[!tip] Tip
>>- Coordination number changes from **6** to **4**.
>>- Geometry changes from **octahedral** to **tetrahedral**.
>>- $\ce{Cl-}$ is a weaker ligand than $\ce{H2O}$ in the spectrochemical series, so **concentrated** $\ce{HCl}$ must be used to drive the exchange.
>>- **Entropy change ($\Delta S_{\text{system}} > 0$):** 5 aqueous reactant particles produce 7 product particles (1 complex + 6 $\ce{H2O}$), making $\Delta S$ positive and driving the endothermic forward reaction at higher temperatures.
