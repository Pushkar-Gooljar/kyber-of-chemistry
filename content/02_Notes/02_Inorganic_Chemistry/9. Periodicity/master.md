# ⚛️ Chapter 9: The Periodic Table & Chemical Periodicity

**Tags:** #chemistry #alevel #cie #9701 #periodicity #inorganic
**Syllabus Focus:** 9.1 Physical Periodicity (Period 3), 9.2 Chemical Periodicity (Period 3), 9.3 Predicting Properties

> [!info] What is Periodicity?
> **Periodicity** is the repeating pattern of physical and chemical properties of elements as you move across the periods of the Periodic Table. In A-Level Chemistry, Period 3 (Na to Ar) is our primary model for understanding these trends.

![Periodic Table](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2021/05/The-Periodic-table_2.png)

## 📉 1. Physical Properties of Period 3 Elements (9.1)

Physical properties are dictated entirely by the **chemical bonding** and **macromolecular structure** of the elements. 

### A. Atomic Radius
* **Trend:** Decreases across the period.
* **Reason:** The **nuclear charge** (number of protons) increases, pulling the electrons in more tightly. The **shielding effect** remains roughly constant because the extra electrons are added to the *same* principal quantum shell.

![Atomic Radius Graph](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Atomic-Radius-Graph.png)

### B. Ionic Radius
* **Trend for Cations (Na⁺ to Si⁴⁺):** Decreases. They are much smaller than their parent atoms (loss of the outer shell = less shielding). The increasing nuclear charge pulls the remaining shell in tighter.
* **Trend for Anions (P³⁻ to Cl⁻):** Decreases. They are larger than their parent atoms (added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion, causing the cloud to expand). Across the non-metals, nuclear charge increases, so the radius shrinks from P³⁻ to Cl⁻.

![Ionic Radius Graph](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Ionic-Radius-Graph.png)

### C. Melting Point
Melting points depend on the type of structure and bonding.
* **Na, Mg, Al (Giant Metallic):** Melting point *increases*. 
  * **Why?** The charge on the metal ion increases (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺) and the number of delocalised electrons increases. This results in a stronger electrostatic attraction (stronger metallic bonding).
* **Si (Giant Molecular/Covalent):** *Highest melting point* in the period. 
  * **Why?** Massive network of strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt the solid.
* **P, S, Cl, Ar (Simple Molecular):** Melting points drop significantly and depend on weak **London dispersion forces** (instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces).
  * **Order:** $S_8 > P_4 > Cl_2 > Ar$. 
  * **Why?** Sulfur exists as $S_8$ rings (more electrons = stronger London forces), while Phosphorus is $P_4$. Argon exists as single atoms.

![Melting Point Graph](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Melting-Point-Graph.png)

### D. Electrical Conductivity
* **Increases** from Na to Al (more delocalised electrons per atom available as charge carriers).
* **Drops heavily** at Si (metalloid/semiconductor – no free electrons, but can conduct under certain conditions).
* **Zero** for P to Ar (non-metals, simple molecules, electrons are localized in covalent bonds).

> [!tip] Examiner Trick
> When asked *why* aluminium has a higher melting point/conductivity than sodium, you **must** mention two factors for full marks: **(1)** Higher charge on the cation ($Al^{3+}$ vs $Na^+$) AND **(2)** A greater number of delocalised electrons in the "sea".

## ⚡ 2. Electronegativity & Bonding Variations (9.2)

> [!abstract] Electronegativity Definition
> The power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond towards itself.

* **Trend:** Increases from Na to Cl.
* **Reason:** Increasing nuclear charge with the same shielding pulls bonding electrons closer.

![Electronegativity Graph](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Electronegativity-Graph.png)

This explains the shift across Period 3:
* **Na to Al:** Low electronegativity $\rightarrow$ form **Giant Metallic** lattices.
* **Si:** Intermediate $\rightarrow$ forms **Giant Covalent** lattice.
* **P to Cl:** High electronegativity $\rightarrow$ form **Simple Molecular** structures (covalent bonds within, weak forces between).

## 🔥 3. Reactions of Period 3 Elements

### A. Reaction with Oxygen
Elements react to form oxides with their highest stable oxidation states (except Sulfur, which mostly forms $SO_2$).

### C. Reaction with Water (Na and Mg only)
* **Sodium (Cold Water):** Vigorous. Melts into a ball, fizzes, hydrogen gas given off. 
  * $2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g)$ (Strongly alkaline, pH 14)
* **Magnesium (Cold Water):** Extremely slow. 
  * $Mg(s) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow Mg(OH)_2(aq) + H_2(g)$ (Weakly alkaline, pH 9-11, due to low solubility of $Mg(OH)_2$)
* **Magnesium (STEAM):** Vigorous reaction.
  * $Mg(s) + H_2O(g) \rightarrow MgO(s) + H_2(g)$

## 🧪 4. Period 3 Oxides & Hydroxides

### Acid / Base Nature of Oxides
The nature of the oxides shifts from **Basic $\rightarrow$ Amphoteric $\rightarrow$ Acidic** as bonding shifts from Ionic $\rightarrow$ Covalent.

| Oxide | Structure/Bonding | Acid/Base Nature | Reaction with Water | pH of solution |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$Na_2O$** | Giant Ionic | Basic | $Na_2O + H_2O \rightarrow 2NaOH(aq)$ | 12 - 14 |
| **$MgO$** | Giant Ionic | Basic | $MgO + H_2O \rightarrow Mg(OH)_2(aq)$ | 8 - 10 |
| **$Al_2O_3$** | Giant Ionic (covalent char.) | **Amphoteric** | Insoluble | - |
| **$SiO_2$** | Giant Covalent | Acidic | Insoluble | - |
| **$P_4O_{10}$** | Simple Molecular | Acidic | $P_4O_{10} + 6H_2O \rightarrow 4H_3PO_4(aq)$ | 1 - 2 |
| **$SO_2$** | Simple Molecular | Acidic | $SO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2SO_3(aq)$ | 1 - 2 |
| **$SO_3$** | Simple Molecular | Acidic | $SO_3 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2SO_4(aq)$ | 1 |

> [!warning] Past Paper Trap: Amphoteric Aluminium Oxide
> You **must** know the equations for $Al_2O_3$ acting as both an acid and a base. This comes up frequently!
> * **As a base (reacting with acid):** $Al_2O_3(s) + 6HCl(aq) \rightarrow 2AlCl_3(aq) + 3H_2O(l)$
> * **As an acid (reacting with hot conc. base):** $Al_2O_3(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H_2O(l) \rightarrow 2NaAl(OH)_4(aq)$ 
> *(Note: The product is sodium aluminate).*

> [!note] Past Paper Trap: $SiO_2$
> $SiO_2$ is insoluble in water. However, it is classified as an **acidic oxide** because it reacts with hot, concentrated alkali:
> $SiO_2(s) + 2NaOH(aq) \rightarrow Na_2SiO_3(aq) + H_2O(l)$

### Hydroxides
* **$NaOH$**: Strong base. $NaOH + HCl \rightarrow NaCl + H_2O$
* **$Mg(OH)_2$**: Weak base (used in indigestion remedies). $Mg(OH)_2 + 2HCl \rightarrow MgCl_2 + 2H_2O$
* **$Al(OH)_3$**: Amphoteric.
  * With Acid: $Al(OH)_3 + 3HCl \rightarrow AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$
  * With Base: $Al(OH)_3 + NaOH \rightarrow NaAl(OH)_4$

## 🌫️ 5. Period 3 Chlorides & Water

The behaviour of chlorides with water depends entirely on their bonding.

| Chloride | Bonding | Structure | Reaction with Water | pH | Observations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$NaCl$** | Ionic | Giant Ionic | **Dissolves** (hydration) | 7.0 | White solid dissolves $\rightarrow$ colourless sol. |
| **$MgCl_2$** | Ionic | Giant Ionic | **Dissolves** (slight hydrolysis) | 6.5 | White solid dissolves $\rightarrow$ colourless sol. |
| **$Al_2Cl_6$** | Covalent | Simple Molec. | **Hydrolyses** highly exothermic | 3.0 | White solid dissolves, **white fumes of HCl** |
| **$SiCl_4$** | Covalent | Simple Molec. | **Hydrolyses** rapidly | 2.0 | **White fumes of HCl**, off-white ppt ($SiO_2$) |
| **$PCl_5$** | Covalent | Simple Molec. | **Hydrolyses** violently | 2.0 | **White fumes of HCl** |

### The Aluminium Chloride Anomaly
Aluminium chloride is unique. At room temperature, solid $AlCl_3$ is a giant ionic lattice. But upon heating, it sublimes and forms **covalent dimers** ($Al_2Cl_6$) featuring coordinate (dative) bonds.

![Aluminium Chloride Dimer](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2023/03/2-1-the-periodic-table---aluminium-chloride-new.png)

**Why does $Al^{3+}$ make water acidic?**
When $AlCl_3$ is added to water, the high charge density of the $Al^{3+}$ ion severely distorts the electron cloud of surrounding water molecules. It causes a water molecule to lose a hydrogen ion ($H^+$), turning the solution acidic.
$[Al(H_2O)_6]^{3+}(aq) \rightleftharpoons [Al(H_2O)_5(OH)]^{2+}(aq) + H^+(aq)$

![Aluminium Hydration](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Aluminium-Hydration.png)

### Hydrolysis of Silicon(IV) Chloride
$SiCl_4(l) + 2H_2O(l) \rightarrow SiO_2(s) + 4HCl(g)$
*The $SiO_2$ forms a white precipitate, and the $HCl$ gas escapes as steamy white fumes.*

## 🔮 6. Predicting Chemical Periodicity (9.3)

Examiners love giving you an unknown element (e.g., Selenium, Germanium, Gallium) and asking you to predict its properties based on its position in the periodic table.

![Flowchart for predicting elements](https://cdn.savemyexams.com/uploads/2020/11/2.1-The-Periodic-Table-Flowchart.png)

**How to approach these questions:**
1. **Identify the Group:** Tells you the number of valence electrons and likely oxidation states.
   * *Example:* Selenium is in Group 16. It will behave like Sulfur.
2. **Identify the block/type:** Metal, Metalloid, or Non-metal?
   * *Example:* Se is a non-metal. It will likely form a simple molecular structure (like $S_8$), have a low melting point, and form acidic oxides ($SeO_2, SeO_3$).
3. **Reactions:** Apply the known period 3 element template.
   * *Reaction with water:* Simple molecules don't react with water.
   * *Reaction with oxygen:* Forms covalent oxides.
   * *Reaction with chlorine:* Forms simple covalent chlorides ($SeCl_2$) that will *hydrolyse* in water to give white fumes of $HCl$.

## 🧠 Expert Examiner Tips & Common Traps

Having analysed decades of past papers (2009-2022), here are the exact places students lose marks:

1. **Dissolving vs. Hydrolysing:**
   * $NaCl$ and $MgCl_2$ **dissolve** in water (physical process, ions simply get hydrated).
   * $SiCl_4$ and $PCl_5$ **hydrolyse** (chemical reaction, bonds break, $HCl$ gas is evolved). Do not use the word "dissolve" for covalent chlorides!
2. **Missing State Symbols:**
   * In periodicity equations, state symbols are often required for the marks. Always include them, especially for the formation of $HCl(g)$ or $SO_2(g)$.
3. **Phosphorus vs. Sulfur Melting Points:**
   * You *must* know that Sulfur ($S_8$) has a **higher** melting point than Phosphorus ($P_4$). Both are simple molecular, but $S_8$ is a larger molecule with more electrons, meaning it has stronger instantaneous dipole-induced dipole forces (London forces).
4. **Silicon Dioxide ($SiO_2$):**
   * Do NOT write $SiO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow \text{something}$. **It does not react with water**. It is a giant covalent structure (like sand/quartz). Its acidic nature is only proven by reacting it with hot, concentrated $NaOH$.
5. **Oxidation Numbers Check:**
   * When asked to state oxidation numbers across period 3 oxides, ensure you include the "+" sign. (e.g., +1, +2, +3, +4, +5, +6).
6. **Aluminium Chloride Bonding:**
   * Solid $AlCl_3$ = Ionic (with covalent character).
   * Gaseous $Al_2Cl_6$ = Covalent (dimer with dative/coordinate bonds).
7. **"Suggest the type of bonding..."**
   * If an unknown chloride melts at a low temperature and reacts violently with water to give pH 2, it is **simple molecular / covalent**. If it melts at $801^\circ C$ and forms a pH 7 solution, it is **giant ionic**.
